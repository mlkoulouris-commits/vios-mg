THE BRAND BOOK — VERSION 2
This document defines what Stochna is, who it’s for, how it behaves, what it says, and how it looks. Everything here has been decided. Nothing here is aspirational.
It is written for two audiences: the people who will build Stochna (owners, staff, partners) and the designer who will give it a visual identity. Both should be able to use this document as a decision-making tool. If something isn’t in here, it probably doesn’t belong.
The document is organized in six parts. Read them in order the first time. After that, use whatever section answers the question you’re asking.
PART 1: WHO WE ARE
Strategic Foundation
1.1 PURPOSE
Why we exist, aside from making money.
Stochna exists to create the social experience that beer and food deserve when they are made together in one place.
Beer is one of the most social things humans make. It is shared, ordered in rounds, talked about across the table, brewed in communities. Yet most places treat beer as inventory — something to stock, list, and sell. They separate it from the food, from the making, from the people around the table.
We take a clear position: when beer is brewed where food is cooked, and both are conceived as one thing, something different happens. People stay longer. Conversations go deeper. The table becomes the center of the evening, not a stop along the way.
Core Belief
We believe beer and food, when made together in the same place, create the conditions for genuine social connection.
This belief is operational. It shapes everything — what we brew, what we cook, how we serve, and how the space works.
What This Purpose Allows
This purpose gives explicit permission to:
Design everything around the table and the social experience it enables
Invest in making things on site rather than sourcing for convenience
Build a place people want to come to with friends, not just for themselves
Enhance what beer can be when it has food designed around it
What This Purpose Refuses
This purpose refuses:
Treating beer as inventory to be listed and sold
Separating the making from the serving
Designing for individual consumption over shared experience
Treating food and beer as two businesses sharing a roof
If an action weakens the connection between beer, food, and the people sharing them, it undermines the reason Stochna exists.
1.2 VISION
Where we want to influence — not where we want to grow.
To elevate the social identity of beer — proving that a brewery can be the center of a city’s social life when brewing and cooking work as one.
This vision is cultural, not commercial. “Elevate” does not imply pretension. It means showing that beer culture — at its best — is about the table, the company, and the making. Not about tap counts, styles listed, or tasting notes performed.
If this vision succeeds, people would increasingly:
Choose a brewery as the place they bring friends for a great evening
Expect beer and food to be designed together, not offered side by side
Value a short list of beers made on site over a long list sourced from elsewhere
See the brewery as a social destination, not just a drinking venue
These are cultural shifts, not aesthetic ones.
1.3 MISSION
What we do every day, regardless of circumstances.
We commit to brewing and cooking in the same place as one integrated system, offering proper choices with care, hosting people as equals, and creating the conditions where good company becomes the point of the evening.
The mission is not a goal or a promise. It is a set of non-negotiable operating commitments that translate belief into repeatable action.
What each commitment means operationally:
Brewing and cooking in the same place — Beer and food are conceived as a single system. Production is not outsourced for convenience. Guests can perceive authorship and process. Place is not a backdrop; it is an ingredient.
Offering proper choices with care — Choice is curated intentionally. Every item must justify its presence. Additions require subtraction. Mastery is valued over novelty. “Proper” means: it earned its place.
Hosting people as equals — No hierarchy between first-timer and regular, between beer expert and someone who just wants something cold. Warmth is structural, not performed.
Creating conditions for good company — The space, service rhythm, and design decisions all serve one outcome: people having a genuinely good time together. This is the measure of success.
What violates the mission:
Outsourcing core products to increase margin
Expanding menus to capture broader demand
Creating environments that work against conversation and shared experience
Treating guests as transactions rather than company
1.4 CORE VALUES
Four behavioral principles. Non-negotiable. Enforced through repetition.
Value 1: We brew and cook as one thing
This value is the foundation. It defines what Stochna makes and how. Without it, we’re a restaurant with beer or a brewery with snacks.
Brewing and cooking as one thing means: the food is designed for the beer. The beer is designed for the food. Spent grain from the brewery goes into the kitchen. Beer goes into recipes. Pairings are not afterthoughts — they are the starting point.
This value requires that we:
Brew on site and cook on site as non-negotiable defaults
Design menu items with specific beers in mind
Use brewery byproducts (spent grain, yeast) in kitchen applications
Keep production visible and honest
Decision test: If a proposal separates the making of beer from the making of food, it fails this value.
Trade-offs accepted: Operational complexity. Higher coordination demands between brewery and kitchen. Scheduling constraints. These trade-offs are not side effects. They are evidence that integration is real.
This value is violated when: Beer and food are developed independently. Spent grain is discarded. Pairings are added after the fact rather than designed in.
Value 2: We offer proper choices, not more choices
This value exists to protect clarity and trust. When everything on the menu earned its place, guests can relax into confidence.
This value requires that we:
Keep menus and beer lists short and legible
Add only when something else is removed
Refuse items that exist only to please everyone
Explain choices rather than expand them
Decision test: If an idea increases choice without increasing meaning, it fails this value.
Trade-offs accepted: Disappointment from variety-seeking guests. Slower menu evolution. Reduced short-term novelty. These trade-offs protect long-term trust and clarity.
This value is violated when: Menus grow to capture demand. Items are added without removal. Novelty replaces mastery.
Value 3: We create conditions for genuine social experience
This value defines the outcome all other decisions serve. Stochna is not in the food business or the beer business. It is in the business of people having a genuinely good time together around beer and food.
This value requires that we:
Design space, sound, and lighting to support conversation
Set a service rhythm that gives people room to be with each other
Allow guests to linger without pressure
Treat the table — not the bar, not the stage, not the screen — as the center of the experience
Decision test: If a proposal works against people connecting with each other, it fails this value.
Trade-offs accepted: Fewer covers per service. Longer average stay per guest. Lower turnover. These trade-offs are not costs — they are evidence that the social experience is working.
This value is violated when: Service pressures guests to hurry. Technology fragments attention. Entertainment competes with conversation. The venue optimizes for throughput.
Value 4: We welcome people as equals
This value ensures that everything we do remains warm, open, and human. Beer knowledge is shared, never used as status. The first-time visitor and the weekly regular receive the same quality of attention.
This value requires that we:
Speak plainly, not technically
Guide without judgment
Share knowledge when asked, never impose it
Treat unfamiliarity as normal, not as a deficit
Decision test: Does this preserve warmth and equality?
Trade-offs accepted: Less opportunity for “insider experience” marketing. Slower development of exclusive community identity. Staff must explain without showing off.
This value is violated when: Regulars receive visibly preferential treatment. Language excludes newcomers. Knowledge becomes a gatekeeping tool.
Decision Filter
Before saying yes to anything, we ask:
Does this strengthen the integration of beer and food?
Does this maintain proper, curated choices?
Does this create better conditions for social experience?
Does this preserve warmth and equality?
If any answer is no, the decision is no.
1.5 NAME & PLACE
What Stochna Means
Stochna Gara was Sofia’s freight station district. Industrial, functional, built to move goods — not to be noticed. The name is a geographic marker, not a brand concept.
Why This Location
The building sits 2 kilometers from Sofia’s city center, in an industrial zone most hospitality avoids. This distance is not a compromise. It is a filter.
People who come here choose to come here. The location removes convenience-seekers and accident traffic. What remains are people who came on purpose — already aligned before they arrive.
How This Reinforces What We Are
The name anchors Stochna to a specific place with working history — freight, production, function. Not heritage as marketing performance. Heritage as operational fact.
We are here because the building allows us to brew and cook on site. The industrial context makes sense for what we do. Making things requires space, equipment, and neighbors who tolerate noise and deliveries at odd hours.
The location could have been hidden or apologized for. Instead, it is named. Stochna Gara is not picturesque. Neither are we.
How the Building Type Matches
Freight stations received materials, processed them, distributed them. That function continues. Bulgarian grain, hops, vegetables arrive. We brew and cook. Beer and food are served. The station connected Sofia to villages and cities in multiple directions. The kitchen works the same way — local ingredients, techniques from elsewhere, people from everywhere. Not a story. A building type doing what it was designed to do.
What This Refuses
This refuses:
Manufactured heritage divorced from actual place
Apologizing for distance from city center
Pretending industrial context is aesthetic choice
Using historical parallel as brand mythology
The building’s original function matches current use. That is observation, not storytelling.

PART 3: WHERE WE STAND
Positioning
3.1 COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE
The landscape below is not a ranking. It is a structural map. We are not trying to beat these places. We are trying to be something none of them are.
The question is not “who is our biggest threat?” but “what model does each one represent, and how is ours fundamentally different?”
The Structural Filter
Every competitor in Sofia’s craft scene can be understood through one question: What does this place optimize for?
Most optimize for one of four things: selection, production, atmosphere, or entertainment. Stochna optimizes for none of these. It optimizes for integrated making and the social experience it creates — which requires a completely different operating logic.
Competitor 1: Kanaal
What they are: Sofia’s pioneering craft beer bar (est. 2011). Central location, strong atmosphere, 12–38 taps, Asian-inspired food, DJ nights, specialty coffee program.
What they optimize for: Curation and atmosphere. They select well and look good doing it.
Structural difference from Stochna: Kanaal curates other people’s work. Stochna makes its own. Kanaal adds stimulation (DJs, events) to create energy. Stochna creates energy through the social experience of fresh beer and food designed together. Kanaal’s aesthetic is designed — exposed brick, Edison bulbs, “hole in the wall” discovery. Stochna’s aesthetic is accumulated — things are there because they’re used.
Where they fall short on their own terms: Service is consistently criticized as unfriendly. The designed aesthetic has aged into “hipster starter kit.” DJ nights fragment conversation. The gap between how it looks and how it feels is growing.
What we share: Both attract culturally discerning guests. Both value quality over mass appeal. Roughly 80% persona overlap.
What we don’t share: They perform craft. We practice it.
Competitor 2: White Stork Republic
What they are: Bulgaria’s first craft brewery (est. 2013). Award-winning beers. 20-tap wall (“largest in Bulgaria”). Arcade, foosball, live music. Massive space. Owns Pivoteka bottle shop.
What they optimize for: Scale and entertainment. Big selection, big space, big events.
Structural difference from Stochna: White Stork doesn’t brew on-site — they proudly state “we don’t own brewing equipment and we don’t care.” They separate making from serving entirely. Their space functions as entertainment venue with beer. Stochna insists on making where we serve, and designs the experience around the table, not the arcade.
Where they fall short on their own terms: The “club-like” atmosphere contradicts craft intimacy. Entertainment (arcade, foosball) competes with their own product for attention. No brewing transparency despite “brewery” identity.
What we share: Both reject mainstream beer culture. Both have strong brand identity.
What we don’t share: They entertain. We host.
Competitor 3: Vitamin B Bar
What they are: Highly popular craft beer pub (est. mid-2010s). Central location, two rooms plus huge garden. 350+ beer selection (7-page bottle list). Knowledgeable staff. No food program.
What they optimize for: Selection depth. They have more beer than anyone. That is the value proposition.
Structural difference from Stochna: Vitamin B offers maximum choice — the exact opposite of our operating logic. Their 7-page bottle list is what Stochna’s curated short list refuses to be. They are product-focused; atmosphere is a side effect of popularity, not a design intention. No food integration at all.
Where they fall short on their own terms: Consistently overcrowded — can’t find seats. No food means shorter visits and less social depth. Selection itself becomes overwhelming. Atmosphere is accidental, not designed.
What we share: Knowledgeable staff who care about beer. Genuine enthusiasm for craft.
What we don’t share: They believe more choice equals more value. We believe proper choices equal more care.
Competitor 4: Sofia Electric Brewing
What they are: Professional micro-brewery (est. 2020). Outside city center. Strong IPA program. Australian brewmaster. Tours available. Separate pop-up taproom downtown.
What they optimize for: Production quality. They make excellent beer. That is the focus.
Structural difference from Stochna: Sofia Electric is production-first — the brewery is the hero, the taproom is secondary. Stochna integrates making and serving as a single system. Sofia Electric separates production facility from serving space (pop-up taproom is downtown, brewery is not). For us, the inseparability of making and serving is the whole point.
Where they fall short on their own terms: Production-focused means the social experience is underdeveloped. Outside city center limits casual visits. Generic “quality craft” positioning. Limited food program.
What we share: Both brew on-site. Both take the craft seriously.
What we don’t share: They make beer that happens to be served. We create social experiences where beer happens to be made.
What No One Else Does
No one in Sofia combines all of the following in a single operating model:
On-site brewing and cooking as a unified system
Food designed for the beer, beer designed for the food
Proper curated choices over abundance
Space and service designed for socializing, not entertainment or display
A social destination worth the trip
To copy this, a competitor would need to integrate their kitchen and brewery operations, reduce their menus, design their food around their beer, and accept that the social experience — not the product list — is the value proposition. Most venues cannot do this without rebuilding from scratch.
This is not a feature advantage. It is a structural one.

3.4 FUNCTIONAL DESCRIPTOR & TAGLINE
Descriptor:
Kitchen Brewery
Not “brewpub.” Not “craft brewery.” Not “brewery-restaurant.” Kitchen Brewery says what Stochna is in the order that matters: kitchen first (human, domestic, making), brewery second (specific craft, not industrial). PART 4: HOW WE BEHAVE
Brand Personality

Archetypes are used here as behavioral forces, not storytelling tropes. They define how the brand behaves emotionally and relationally over time, ensuring consistency without scripts.
Brand Archetype: The Outlaw (Primary — 70%)
Not the chaotic or provocative Outlaw. The principled one.
What this means: The brand rejects dominant systems that treat beer as inventory, food as fuel, and hospitality as transaction. It sets its own rules calmly and consistently. It does not seek permission, approval, or mass appeal.
How this shows up:
Refusal to expand the menu under pressure
Insistence on integrated brewing and cooking when separation would be easier
Calm confidence in proper choices over abundance
Lack of explanatory marketing or justification
The Outlaw stance protects the brand from conformity and erosion.
Brand Archetype: The Everyman (Secondary — 30%)
To balance the Outlaw and prevent elitism.
What this means: Everyone is treated as an equal. Warmth replaces performance. Knowledge is shared, not used as status. The place feels like somewhere you’d bring anyone.
How this shows up:
Staff speak plainly, not technically
Guests are guided without judgment
The space feels inclusive without being generic
A first-timer feels as welcome as a regular
The Everyman ensures the Outlaw remains human and the social experience stays open.
The Creator (Operational Engine)
Present through what is made, not through personality.
What this means: The integration of brewing and cooking is the creative act. The Creator shows in the products — the beer, the food, the combinations — not in messaging or brand voice.
How this shows up:
Beer brewed with specific dishes in mind
Food incorporating brewery byproducts (spent grain, beer as ingredient)
Pairings that reveal something neither beer nor food does alone
Visible process — guests see the making
The Creator is Stochna’s engine. It runs through the kitchen and the brewery, not through the brand.
The Archetypal Relationship
The relationship between brand and audience is asymmetrical and intentional:
The brand takes the risk of doing things differently (Outlaw)
The guest receives warmth, belonging, and a great evening (Everyman)
The making gives the whole thing substance (Creator)
This is why the proper choices feel trustworthy, not restrictive. Why the distance feels worth it. Why the evening feels different from anywhere else.
Archetypal Boundaries
To remain coherent, Stochna must avoid drifting into:
Performer — seeking attention or spectacle
Sage — lecturing, moralizing, or educating uninvited
Lover — seduction, indulgence, or sensory excess
Such shifts confuse the relationship and undermine trust.
Archetypal Consistency Rule
When in doubt: Are we creating the best conditions for people to enjoy beer, food, and each other’s company — while remaining warm and equal with everyone inside?
If the answer is no, the action is misaligned.
4.2 BRAND CHARACTER: “THE QUIET REFUSER”
If Stochna were a person.
A 38-year-old who runs their kitchen the way they run their life — on their own terms, without explanation. But their door is always open, and there’s always room at the table.
They left a restaurant job not because they had a vision, but because they couldn’t tolerate one more shortcut. They didn’t announce it. They just stopped showing up and started cooking at home. Friends started coming over. Then friends of friends.
Their apartment smells like bread dough and hops. The door is usually open. When you arrive, they don’t stop what they’re doing to greet you. They nod, gesture at the counter — “there’s beer, glasses are there” — and keep stirring. Within ten minutes you’re tearing herbs alongside them. You never felt “welcomed” because you never felt like an outsider.
They don’t own a microwave. Not as a statement. It just never occurred to them to buy one. When someone asks why, they look mildly confused — “I have a stove.” That’s the whole answer. No philosophy. No lecture. Just a person whose choices are so internally consistent that alternatives don’t register.
At a dinner party, they’re the one who makes sure everyone has a full glass and a good seat. They listen, ask one good question, and then say something so plain and direct that the whole table goes quiet for a second. Not because it was clever. Because it was true.
They have strong opinions about shortcuts, noise, and rushed meals. But these opinions live in what they do, not what they say. The small menu isn’t minimalism — it’s focus. Everything on the table was made from scratch because that’s the only way they know how to cook.
If you get it, they’re glad. If you don’t, they don’t adjust. Not out of stubbornness or superiority — they just can’t operate differently. This is the only way they know how to cook, brew, and host. And their table is always full.
Why This Character Works
Outlaw shows as quiet refusal by nature, not performed rebellion
Everyman shows as immediate equality through action — you’re chopping herbs, not being hosted
Creator lives as the thing they happen to be doing, not as identity
The character doesn’t explain or justify — matches “experienced, not told”
The open door + no formal welcome = warmth replaces performance
The table is always full — this is a social person, not a hermit with principles

4.5 VOICE BY CONTEXT
Explaining How Things Work (Website, Menu, First-Time Visitors)
“We brew the beer here. We cook the food here. They go together because they come from the same place. The menu is short because we’d rather do a few things properly.”
States facts, not philosophy. “We’d rather” is preference, not mandate. Sounds like a person, not a brand.
Describing Beer or Food (Menu, Staff, Social)
“This one has local honey in it. You’ll taste it at the end, after the hops settle. Goes well with the bread.”
Technical information delivered casually. Sensory without being precious. Pairing suggested naturally. Short sentences. Done.
When Someone Questions a Constraint
“Yeah, there’s no WiFi. Honestly, we just never set it up. There’s a café on the corner if you need to check something. We’ll be here.”
Acknowledges without apologizing. Doesn’t make it a philosophy. Offers alternative without judgment. “We’ll be here” — warmth through presence.
Welcoming First-Timers
“First time? Good. Sit anywhere. Beer list is short — if you’re not sure, I’ll pour you something. Food comes when it’s ready. No rush.”
Not excited, not ceremonial. Practical info, not a philosophy tour. Personal, decisive, equal.
Social Media
“Bread’s in. Beer’s cold. Door’s open. Tuesday.”
No call to action. No selling. Reads like a text from a friend. States what’s happening.
“Someone asked why the menu only has six items. Because seven was too many.”
Dry humor without cruelty. No lecture about philosophy.
Press/Media
“We brew and cook in the same space, serve a proper menu, and the food is designed around the beer. People come with friends, stay a while, usually come back. We think the two things are connected.”
Facts first. Lets the data imply the philosophy. “We think” — certain but not arrogant.

Messaging Architecture
5.1 HOW MESSAGING WORKS HERE
Core messages are not slogans, taglines, or fixed pieces of copy. They are the underlying themes that structure all communication, behavior, and experience.
The system is composed of:
Primary Core Messages — market relevance and value (why should I care?)
Secondary Core Messages — depth, substance, belief (why does this matter?)
Story Framework — narrative architecture (how meaning accumulates)
Micro-Message Blocks — touchpoint translation (what must be understood at each moment)
5.2 PRIMARY CORE MESSAGES
These answer the first question in the customer’s mind: Why is this place relevant to me, and why should I care?
Message 1 — Beer and Food, Made Together
For: People who value authenticity and want to know where things come from.
Pain: Most breweries don’t cook seriously. Most restaurants don’t brew. The two worlds are kept apart.
Understanding we want to create: Stochna brews and cooks as one system. The food is designed for the beer. The beer is designed for the food. Both are made here.
Emotional value: Trust, excitement about combinations, the feeling that someone actually thought about this.
Competitors say: We have great beer. We have food too. We say: We cook for beer. We brew for food. They don’t make sense apart.
Where this message lives: Visible production. Menu pairings. Staff explaining how a dish was designed for a specific beer. The tagline itself.
Message 2 — Proper Choices, Greater Clarity
For: People who feel relief when options are curated rather than overwhelming.
Pain: Too much choice creates confusion and shallow engagement.
Understanding we want to create: Everything on this menu earned its place. The proper list is the whole list.
Emotional value: Confidence in what is offered. Permission to trust.
Competitors say: Large menus and endless options as generosity. We say: We offer only what’s proper — and that creates meaning and care.
Where this message lives: Short menu. Curated beer list. Staff guiding choices confidently. No daily specials for novelty’s sake.
Message 3 — A Place Worth the Trip
For: People deciding where to spend an evening with friends.
Pain: Most places closer to home are fine but not special. Worth the trip means something genuinely different.
Understanding we want to create: Stochna is where you go when you want a great evening, not just a meal. The distance is part of the filter — everyone here chose to be here.
Emotional value: Belonging to something purposeful. An evening that delivers.
Competitors say: Central location, convenient, something for everyone. We say: 2km from center. Worth it. Everyone here came on purpose.
Where this message lives: The experience itself. Word-of-mouth. How guests describe it to friends.
5.3 SECONDARY CORE MESSAGES

Message 4 — The Table Is the Center

Understanding we want to create: Everything at Stochna — pacing, portions, pairings — is designed for the table, not the individual.

5.4 STORY FRAMEWORK
The story framework defines how Stochna is understood over time. The guest is the hero, the brand is the guide, the story is experienced — not told.
1. The World As It Is (Shared Context)
Beer culture in most cities has split into extremes. On one side: mass-produced, anonymous, no character. On the other: fetishized, overcomplicated, intimidating. In between, there’s a gap — places where beer is genuinely social, where food and beer work together, where you don’t need to be an expert or a snob to have a great evening.
2. The Problem (Social Reality)
Most places designed around beer either focus on selection (overwhelming), entertainment (distracting), or production (beer-first, people-second). The social potential of beer — the rounds, the table, the friends, the conversation — is treated as a side effect, not the main event.
People leave nights out having consumed, not having connected.
3. The Cost of Not Changing
If beer culture stays split between industrial anonymity and craft pretension, the social heart of beer — which has existed for centuries — fades. Places designed around connection become rarer. Evenings become consumption events, not social ones.
4. The Alternative (A Different Possibility)
Another kind of brewery is possible. A place where beer and food are made together, where the menu is proper and every item has a reason, where the evening revolves around the table and the people at it.
People stay longer without planning to. They order another round because the conversation is good. They bring friends next time.
5. The Guide (Role of Stochna)
Stochna does not position itself as the hero. It acts as a guide — experienced, principled, warm. The guide’s role is to create conditions, design great combinations, and make the social experience effortless.
Stochna does this through design and behavior, not explanation.
6. The Way (How It Works)
The path from problem to alternative is enabled through: brewing and cooking on site as one system, proper curated choices, service pacing that supports the table, warm and equal hosting, and a space designed for conversation.
These are not features. They are structural commitments.
7. The Transformation (After State)
After an evening at Stochna, the guest feels: well-fed, well-watered, socially energized, and inclined to return with different friends.
“You have to try this place.” That sentence is the whole transformation, condensed.
5.5 MICRO-MESSAGE BLOCKS
Block 1 — Arrival & First Contact
Anchors: “A Place Worth the Trip” + “Meeting the Guide”
Touchpoint: Arrival, door, host greeting, first seconds inside.
Customer state: Made the trip. Checking whether it was worth it. Scanning for cues about what kind of place this is.
Tension to resolve: Was this trip worth it?
Understanding we must create: You’re in the right place. Relax. It’s going to be a good evening.
Non-verbal expression: Warm greeting. Relaxed pacing. The smell of food and beer. Visible production. People already having a good time.
Verbal expression: Language that welcomes without ceremony. Practical, warm, immediate.
Success signal: Guests relax within the first minute. Physical ease. First smile.
Failure modes: Guests feel uncertain, rushed, or unwelcome. The space feels empty or over-designed.
Block 2 — Ordering & Choice
Anchors: “Proper Choices, Greater Clarity” + “Beer and Food, Made Together”
Touchpoint: Menu encounter, first ordering interaction, staff recommendation.
Customer state: Scanning for how this place works. Curious about the integration. Deciding whether to trust the curation.
Tension to resolve: Is this proper menu a sign of care or a sign of limitation?
Understanding we must create: Every item earned its place. The proper list is the whole list. And the beer goes with the food on purpose.
Non-verbal expression: Clean, legible menu. Confident staff body language. No upselling behavior.
Verbal expression: Language that guides without prescribing. Pairing suggestions delivered naturally. “This goes well with that” — not a lecture.
Success signal: Guest orders with confidence. Tries a pairing. Asks about the beer.
Failure modes: Guest feels the menu is incomplete. Staff can’t explain why items were chosen or how beer and food connect.
Block 3 — Making & Explanation (Beer / Food)
Anchors: “Beer and Food, Made Together” + “The Way”
Touchpoint: Beer and food explanation at first pour / first plate. Visible production moments. Guest questions about origin or process.
Customer state: Curious but casual. Open to learning if it feels human. Sensitive to pretension or lectures.
Tension to resolve: Is this real making, or performance?
Understanding we must create: What you are drinking and eating was made here, together, on purpose. The spent grain in this bread came from this morning’s brew.
Non-verbal expression: Visible production cues. Calm, practiced handling. No theatrical staging.
Verbal expression: Language that speaks from experience, not scripts. Plain words. Depth follows interest.
Success signals: Guests ask follow-up questions. Share a tasting with the table. Take a photo of the combination, not just the beer.
Failure modes: Explanations turn into lectures. Integration feels forced or performed.
Block 4 — The Evening Unfolds
Anchors: “The Table Is the Center” + “Transformation”
Touchpoint: Mid-visit. After first round, between plates, natural pauses. The decision to stay for another round.
Customer state: Settled in. Conversation flowing. Time becomes elastic.
Tension to resolve: Should we stay or move on?
Understanding we must create: Stay. There’s no rush, no pressure, and the next round goes well with what’s coming from the kitchen.
Non-verbal expression: No hovering or clearing pressure. Pacing supports conversation. Another beer arrives when the table is ready for it.
Verbal expression: Language that suggests without pushing. “There’s a darker one that goes well with what’s coming next — want to try it?”
Success signals: Guests initiate the next order themselves. Conversation continues uninterrupted. The table stays full and alive.
Failure modes: Staff check in too frequently. Energy feels managed rather than natural. Urgency implied.
Block 5 — Departure & Memory Formation
Anchors: “A Place Worth the Trip” + “The New World”
Touchpoint: Settling the bill. Final exchange. Walking out.
Customer state: Full, socially satisfied, buzzing slightly from beer and good conversation. Aware they had a better evening than expected.
Tension to resolve: Was this a one-time thing, or my new place?
Understanding we must create: Come back. Bring someone. There’s always something new from the brewery.
Non-verbal expression: No rushing the payment. Warm, human farewell. Genuine.
Verbal expression: “See you next time” — not as a script, as a prediction.
Success signals: Guests say “we’re coming back.” Mention bringing friends. Take a photo of the space on the way out.
Failure modes: Exit feels procedural. Loyalty or reviews requested. The evening ends on a transactional note.
PART 6: HOW WE LOOK
Visual Identity Direction
This section is written for the designer. It describes what Stochna should look and feel like — not in specifics (that’s the designer’s job) but in principles, direction, and hard boundaries.
6.1 OVERALL AESTHETIC: “Kitchen That Happens to Brew”
The space should feel like someone’s kitchen expanded — functional, worn-in, accumulated over time. The brewery equipment is there because brewing happens here, not because it looks impressive. The kitchen is visible because cooking happens here, not for theater.
This is not a designed aesthetic. It is closer to a place that wasn’t designed at all. It just became what it is because of what happens inside it.
What This Looks Like
Functional objects in their natural position — equipment where it’s used, not displayed
Surfaces that show wear from actual use, not distressed-for-effect
A sense of accumulation — things added because they were needed, not for cohesion
Domestic scale details: kitchen towels, real shelving, working utensils visible
Comfortable imperfection: a chip in the counter, a stool that doesn’t match
Temperature — literally and emotionally warm; feels inhabited and alive with people
What This Does Not Look Like
Industrial chic (exposed ductwork as feature, steel-and-concrete minimalism)
Rustic farmhouse (burlap, mason jars, barn wood)
Hipster brewery (Edison bulbs, chalkboard menus, reclaimed-everything)
Scandinavian minimal (too clean, too curated, too intentional-looking)
Gastropub polish (too refined, too hospitality-industry)
“Designed to look undesigned” — the worst trap. Trying to look effortless is still trying.
The Test
If a design choice could appear in a “Top 10 Craft Brewery Interiors” article, it’s probably wrong for Stochna.
The space should look like it would confuse an interior design magazine — not ugly, not beautiful, just there. And full of people having a good time.
6.2 COLOR DIRECTION
Colors emerge from materials, not from brand theory.
The palette should feel:
Warm but muted — kitchen warmth, not brand warmth
Like materials, not like colors — the brown of actual wood, the gray of actual concrete, the amber of actual beer
Unselected — as if the colors arrived with the materials, not from a palette
Direction for the designer: The color palette should emerge from the materials palette. If the space has wooden surfaces, the palette includes that wood’s actual tone. If there’s concrete, that concrete’s actual gray. The “brand colors” are the colors of the things that are already there.
Accent approach: One warm accent — something that says “this is the kitchen, this is where the heat is.” Not a rebel red. Something more like the color of bread crust, or the amber of beer held to light, or the glow of a pilot light. Something you’d see in an actual kitchen.
Color don’ts:
No neon or fluorescent
No pastels (too designed)
No pure black or pure white (too graphic)
No “brand red” or “brand green” as feature colors (too deliberate)
No color-coding system (too organized — this is a kitchen, not a brand)
6.3 TYPOGRAPHY DIRECTION
The Quiet Refuser doesn’t have a font. They have handwriting.
The font is not a personality statement. It’s a tool.
Primary Font (Logo, Headlines, Signage)
Requirements:
Readable first. Characterful second.
Not aggressive, not delicate
Could plausibly be hand-lettered on a kitchen chalkboard — but clean enough to work printed
Feels like it belongs to a person, not a brand
Works in English and Bulgarian Cyrillic (non-negotiable)
Personality: Sturdy, not stylish. Slightly imperfect without performing imperfection. The typographic equivalent of plain-spoken.
Not: Script/calligraphic. Ultra-modern geometric sans. Distressed/grunge. Ornate display. Generic rounded sans-serif.
Secondary Font (Body Copy, Menus, Materials)
Requirements:
Highly readable in dim light (operational requirement)
Clean without being cold
Professional enough for press, warm enough for menus
Full character set (Latin + Cyrillic)
Multiple weights (Regular, Bold minimum)
A good serif or a warm sans-serif. Not a statement, just readable.
Usage Principles
Minimal type hierarchy: one headline level, one body, maybe a caption. That’s it.
No decorative use of type — type is for reading, not atmosphere
Generous spacing — let text breathe
Left-aligned default — centered text is for ceremonies; we’re not ceremonial
6.4 MATERIALS DIRECTION
Materials are chosen by the work, not by the brand. The brand expression is a side effect.
Primary Materials
WOOD — Present because kitchens and bars have always used wood. Should look used: knife marks, heat stains, ring marks from glasses. Not one species or finish — different surfaces accumulated over time.
STEEL/METAL — Present because brewing equipment is steel and kitchen equipment is metal. Functional, not decorative.
STONE/CONCRETE — If architecturally present, leave honest. Functional surfaces only.
CERAMIC/STONEWARE — Serving vessels should feel handmade, heavy, functional. Like dishes in someone’s kitchen — not matched sets, but things that work. Local pottery preferred where available.
Secondary Materials
Textiles: Linen, cotton — kitchen towels, not napkins-as-brand-touchpoints. Glass: Clear, functional — for drinking from and seeing through. Leather: If present, old and soft — already worn in. Paper: Practical — not heavy card stock, not kraft paper.
Materials to Avoid
Anything that exists only for atmosphere
Anything new made to look old
Anything referencing “brewery aesthetic” as a genre (hop sacks, grain bags, barrel heads)
Edison bulbs, chalkboard anything, mason jars, exposed filament, industrial pendant lights as statements
The Materials Test
Point to any object. Ask: “Why is this here?” If the answer is “because it looks good” or “because it creates atmosphere” — remove it. If the answer is “because we use it” or “because it was already here” — it stays.

6.7 DESIGNER BRIEF SUMMARY
What This Brand Is
Stochna is a Kitchen Brewery in Sofia, Bulgaria. Beer is brewed on site. Food is cooked on site. They are conceived as one integrated thing. The food is designed for the beer, the beer is designed for the food. The menu is proper — everything earned its place. People come with friends, stay a long time, and come back. The tagline is “Cook for beer. Brew for food.”
What the Brand Personality Feels Like
The Quiet Refuser. Not loud, not bold, not trying to be noticed. A person who runs their kitchen the way they run their life — on their own terms, without explaining why. Their door is always open and their table is always full. Warmth comes from equality and genuine hospitality, not from performance.
What the Visual Identity Must Achieve
Feel like a place, not a brand. The identity should look like it belongs on a building, a menu, a coaster. Not on a pitch deck.
Feel accumulated, not designed. The quality of things that arrived over time — not a coordinated launch.
Feel domestic, not commercial. Kitchen scale, not venue scale.
Feel certain, not loud. Quiet confidence. The visual equivalent of someone who doesn’t raise their voice.
Feel social, not solitary. This is a place full of people, not a temple of craft.
Feel honest. No material, color, or typographic choice should exist for atmosphere. Everything earns its place through function.
Archetype Expression in Visual Terms
Outlaw (70%): Shows as absence — things not there. No clutter, no decoration, no trends, no industry conventions. The visual rebellion is in what’s missing.
Everyman (30%): Shows as warmth and approachability — nothing intimidating, exclusive-looking, or requiring knowledge to enter. The door looks open. The table has room.
Creator (engine): Shows through products — the beer, the bread, the plates. Visible in the space, not in the brand identity.
Deliverables
Primary mark (typographic, English + Cyrillic)
Secondary mark (icon/stamp if needed — not mandatory)
Type system (primary + secondary fonts with usage rules)
Color system (derived from materials, not brand theory)
Basic applications (signage, menu, coaster, business card, social media avatar)
Simple style guide (keep it short — if longer than 10 pages, it’s overdesigned)
Success Metric
A person walks past Stochna. The sign tells them the name. The visible interior tells them the rest — people eating, drinking, talking. The identity got out of the way and let the place speak.
If the logo disappeared tomorrow and only the sign text remained, the brand would still work. That’s how little the visual identity should need to carry.
