## 2.2 Stage 2 — Customer Journey Map


## 2.2.1 What this stage does

Stage 2 produces the map. The map translates the brand into the moments where the brand has to act. It answers three questions: where does the brand show up across the customer's arc, what does it have to do at each of those points, and which moments carry disproportionate weight in the customer's memory of the experience.

The map sits between two stages that do not directly communicate with each other. The brand book establishes who the brand is, what role it plays in the customer's life, what its personality expression looks and sounds like. The journey design specifies the precise behaviour staff perform at each moment. Without the map, the design is a series of unconnected stylistic decisions. With the map, the design has a centre, a sequence, and a hierarchy.

A common misunderstanding: the journey map is not a customer-emotion diagram, and it is not a touchpoint inventory. Customer-emotion diagrams are decorative — they say the customer feels excited at arrival and satisfied at departure, which is true of every hospitality experience that is not failing. Touchpoint inventories are exhaustive — they list every point at which the customer encounters the brand, which is useful for audit but not for design. The journey map is neither. It is a strategic instrument that names where the brand has to do specific work, what work it has to do, and where it should deliberately do nothing.

The map is shorter than people expect. A complete journey map fits on one or two pages. The discipline of fitting it on two pages is itself part of the work: a map that cannot be read at a glance is a map that will not be referenced, and a map that is not referenced has failed its purpose.

The output of Stage 2 is the map itself, plus a short companion document — the Reasoning Note — that explains the placement decisions behind each moment of disproportionate weight. The Reasoning Note is read once, by VIOS and by the founder, to confirm that the map's strategic choices are defensible. After that, the map travels and the Reasoning Note is archived.


## 2.2.2 Method — building the journey map

The method is six moves, run in order. The first move is the intake — confirming what Stage 1 has produced. The next five are the construction of the map itself: establish the arc, identify the moments, assign weight, place the brand at each moment, name the silent moments. The map closes with a coherence test that sends the practice back to any earlier move that no longer holds.


### 2.2.2.1 Inputs from Stage 1

Stage 2 begins with a deliberate intake of the brand book. The practice cannot map the journey of a brand it has not internalised. The intake is short but disciplined.

Six elements of the brand book are read in sequence and held available throughout Stage 2:

Audience Persona, all four layers. The map is built for this specific persona — their before-state emotions, their pain points, their needs, the archetypal mix that shapes how they receive an experience. The persona is the customer in the journey map; the journey is theirs to travel.

Brand Role and Connection statement. The Role determines the brand's relational position at every moment. A Guide brand is alongside the customer; a Defender brand stands in front; a Peer brand is beside; a Leader brand is ahead. The Role is the answer to the question "how does the brand show up here" at every single moment of the map.

Brand Archetypal Mix. The Core and Influencer archetypes inform the character of the brand's actions. The same Role (Guide) played by a Sage brand looks different from the same Role played by an Everyman brand.

Brand Personality Expression. The eight dimensions — physical appearance, dress and style, accessories, language, tone, volume, attitudes, beliefs — are the brand's behavioural vocabulary. The journey map points to which of these dimensions are dominant at which moments.

Differentiator and Positioning Statement. These tell the map where the brand must do the work that no competitor does. The moments where the differentiator becomes visible are usually high-weight moments by definition.

Core Message Blocks and Story Framework. These tell the map what the brand is communicating at each moment, beneath the practical interaction. The Story Framework in particular shapes the arc: the customer is the hero, and the journey map is the brand's plot through the hero's experience.

Sufficient intake: all six elements read and held as working context. The intake is documented in a one-page summary that opens the engagement file for Stage 2.


### 2.2.2.2 Establish the arc

The arc is the shape of the customer's experience from first contact to last. For a hospitality operation, the arc typically begins before the customer arrives — at the moment of decision to come, or the moment a reservation is made — and extends past the moment of departure into whatever post-visit experience the brand intends.

Establishing the arc is the act of naming its phases. A typical hospitality arc has between four and eight phases. Examples of typical phases: decision to come, arrival, seating, ordering, service, payment, departure, post-visit. Each phase is a section of the customer's experience that hangs together — a stretch of time and attention during which the customer is doing one kind of thing.

The discipline of this move is to identify the phases that this specific brand's customer actually travels, not the phases of a generic restaurant journey. A brand whose Role is Defender against a hostile category may have a distinct pre-arrival phase where the customer is overcoming category resistance to come at all. A brand whose differentiator is at the bar — beer programme, cocktail programme, conversational ordering — may have a bar phase that is separate from the dining phase and treated with separate logic.

The arc is drawn as a horizontal line on the working page. Each phase is marked along the line with its name and its approximate duration as a fraction of the total experience. The line is not yet populated with moments; the arc is the skeleton on which moments will hang.

Sufficient arc: between four and eight phases, named specifically to this brand's customer, with durations marked and the line readable at a glance.


### 2.2.2.3 Identify the moments

A moment is a single point in the arc where the brand has to do something specific. Moments are smaller than phases — a single phase usually contains three to eight moments. Moments are the unit of the journey map's work.

Moments are identified by walking through the arc, phase by phase, and asking three questions at each step: what does the customer do here, what does the brand do here, and what is at stake if either of them does it badly. Each answer that is not "nothing" identifies a moment.

Two kinds of moments are captured. Operational moments are the standard interactions — greeting at the door, seating, taking the order, delivering food, presenting the bill, farewell. Brand moments are the moments where the brand's specific identity — the Role, the Archetypal Mix, the Differentiator — comes into play. Many moments are both operational and brand at once. A few are purely operational; a few are purely brand.

The full inventory is captured first, without weight or judgement. Some maps end with twenty moments, some with fifty. The total count is not the point. What matters is that no moment in which the brand has to act has been left off the map.

Sufficient identification: all moments named, in sequence, within their phase, with the customer's action and the brand's action stated for each.


### 2.2.2.4 Assign weight

Not all moments are equal. A few moments carry disproportionate weight in the customer's memory of the experience. These are the moments where the journey design will invest the most attention and where the brand's identity must be most legible.

Weight is assigned by drawing on two principles from the behavioural economics library (Part III, section 3.1.3) and one principle from the brand strategy.

The first principle is peak-end, grounded in the deeper framework of the two selves (Part III, section 3.1.4). The customer's experiencing self lives every moment of the meal; the customer's remembering self constructs a memory after the fact. The remembering self is what decides whether to return, and the remembering self is governed by two rules: peak-end (memory is well predicted by the average of the peak moment and the end moment) and duration neglect (the duration of the experience has almost no effect on the remembered evaluation). Together, these mean a meal of any length is remembered chiefly by its peak and its end. The map identifies both explicitly: which moment is the peak, which moment is the end. These two moments deserve the highest weight by default, and the journey design will spend disproportionate effort on getting them right.

The second principle is the moment-of-decision concentration. Moments where the customer is making a decision — what to order, what to drink, whether to stay for dessert, whether to come back — concentrate cognitive load and emotional weight. They are usually high-weight moments. They are also where behavioural economics is most useful in the design stage that follows.

The third principle is the brand's own logic. Some moments are high-weight because the brand has decided they are. A brand whose Role is Defender treats the moment of arrival — the threshold — as high-weight because that is the moment the customer crosses from the hostile category into the brand's protection. A brand whose Differentiator is at the bar treats every bar moment as high-weight regardless of where it sits in the arc. The brand strategy from Stage 1 is consulted at this move to surface these brand-specific weights.

A fourth consideration, drawn from Gladwell's treatment of rapid cognition and from Part III section 3.2.3, governs the weight of the threshold moment specifically. Customers thin-slice the brand within seconds of entering; the snap judgement formed at the threshold anchors everything that follows in the experience. The threshold is therefore a default high-weight moment in any hospitality journey, regardless of where it sits in the arc or how brief it appears to be. The brand is being judged at the threshold whether the design recognises it or not; the discipline is to make the threshold deliberate.

Each moment is assigned a weight tier: HIGH, MEDIUM, or LOW. The proportions matter. A map where most moments are HIGH is a map without a real hierarchy; the design stage will lose discipline. A typical map has three to seven HIGH moments out of twenty to fifty total, with the rest distributed across MEDIUM and LOW.

Sufficient weighting: every moment tiered HIGH, MEDIUM, or LOW, with the peak and end identified, and the HIGH count restrained to a meaningful minority.


### 2.2.2.5 Place the brand at each moment

Once moments and weights are settled, the brand is placed at each moment. This is the move where Stage 1's outputs do their work.

For each moment, four fields are captured:


#### Brand intention

What does the brand want the customer to feel, understand, or experience at this moment? The intention is drawn from the brand's Connection statement, its Core Message Blocks, and the position of the moment in the Story Framework. Stated as a single sentence.


#### Brand role-in-moment

How does the brand show up here, given its Brand Role from Stage 1? A Guide brand at the seating moment is gesturing, advising, helping the customer make a choice they will be happy with. A Defender brand at the seating moment is protecting the customer from a bad seat — the brand chooses the table the customer would not have known to ask for. A Peer brand at the seating moment is sitting alongside the customer's decision — sit anywhere, take the corner one if you want. The Role does not change; how the Role expresses at this specific moment does.


#### Dominant expression dimension

Which of the eight Personality Expression dimensions from Stage 1 is dominant at this moment? Most moments are dominated by one or two dimensions. The greeting at the door is dominated by Tone of Voice and Attitudes. The presentation of the menu is dominated by Language and Vocabulary. The room itself, working in the background, is dominated by Physical Appearance and Accessories. Naming the dominant dimension at each moment tells the journey design stage what to invest in.


#### Customer target state

Where on the audience's emotional arc is the customer at this moment? The Audience Persona's before-state and after-state emotions are the endpoints; this field locates the moment between them. The customer at the moment of arrival is at or near the before-state; the customer at the moment of farewell should be at or near the after-state. The moments between are points on the arc, and the journey design will work to move the customer along it.

Sufficient placement: four fields completed for every moment in the inventory, with extra attention to the HIGH-weight moments where these fields will be elaborated into full designs in Stage 3.


### 2.2.2.6 Name the silent moments

A silent moment is a moment in the arc where the brand has deliberately chosen to do nothing. Silent moments are not absences; they are designed choices. The map names them explicitly so the journey design stage knows where not to intervene.

Silent moments are usually located in three places. The first is between high-weight moments — the customer needs space to absorb what just happened, and the brand interrupting that space damages the experience. The second is during private customer activity — eating, conversing, reading the menu — where presence from the brand reads as hovering. The third is in transitions — the walk from the door to the table, the wait between courses — where over-design creates choreography the customer can feel.

Silent moments are not the same as low-weight moments. A low-weight moment may still require the brand to do something small; a silent moment requires the brand to do nothing. Marking silence as a deliberate decision protects it from being accidentally filled by staff or operations who default to action.

Sufficient naming: silent moments marked on the map, with the reason for the silence stated. The journey design stage receives them as instructions: at this moment, the brand is absent on purpose.


### 2.2.2.7 Close the map

The map is closed with a coherence test that runs across the whole document. The test asks five questions.

Does the arc reflect this specific brand's customer, or has it defaulted to a generic hospitality arc? If generic, the arc is revised.

Are all moments named, or are there places in the arc where the brand has to act and the map says nothing? If gaps exist, the inventory is reopened.

Does the weight distribution have real hierarchy, or are too many moments HIGH? If the HIGH count is excessive, the weights are renegotiated.

Does each placement at each moment derive from the brand book, or are some moments populated with intentions the brand book does not support? If yes, the placement is rewritten or the brand book is questioned.

Do the silent moments read as choices, or do they read as gaps? If the latter, they are reconsidered.

The map is closed when all five tests pass. The Reasoning Note is written at this point — a short companion document that explains why the peak is where it is, why the HIGH moments are HIGH, and why the silent moments are silent. The Reasoning Note is the founder's read-once document; the map is the working document that travels.


## 2.2.3 Template — the journey map

The journey map is produced on a standard template that imposes the structure of the method on the output. The template is one or two pages, landscape orientation, designed to be read at a glance.

The template has five horizontal bands, read top to bottom for each moment.


#### Band 1 — The arc

The top band shows the arc as a horizontal line across the page, with phases marked along its length. The phases are labelled and their durations are visually proportionate to the time the customer spends in each. Phase names sit above the line; phase durations sit below.


#### Band 2 — The moments

The second band lists the moments along the arc, in sequence, each positioned at its location in time within its phase. Moments are coded with short identifiers (M1, M2, etc.) for cross-reference. Each moment is named in three or four words: "Threshold crossed," "Menu presented," "First taste," "Bill arrives," "Farewell."


#### Band 3 — Weight

The third band shows the weight tier of each moment as a visual indicator — a filled block or coloured cell at HIGH, MEDIUM, or LOW. The peak is marked with a distinct icon; the end is marked with another. The visual treatment makes the hierarchy of the map readable without text.


#### Band 4 — Brand at the moment

The fourth band, the largest, captures the brand placement for each moment in four compressed fields: Brand Intention (one sentence), Brand Role-in-Moment (one phrase), Dominant Expression Dimension (one or two words), Customer Target State (one phrase). HIGH-weight moments are expanded vertically to fit fuller text; LOW-weight moments are compressed to a single line.


#### Band 5 — Silent moments

The fifth band, narrow, runs along the bottom of the page and marks the silent moments with a visual indicator placed at the corresponding point in the arc. Silent moments are not given the four-field treatment; they are marked as silent and the reason for the silence is captured in the Reasoning Note.


#### On readability

The template is designed to be read at a glance by a non-specialist reader. A founder, a senior staff member, a designer, an architect — all should be able to look at the map and understand what the brand is doing across the customer's experience without needing it explained. If the map is unreadable at a glance, the template has been overloaded or the moments are too granular.

The Reasoning Note, by contrast, is written to be read sequentially. It is structured by phase and follows the map's order. It is the document that explains decisions; the map is the document that holds them.


## 2.2.4 Toolkit

The toolkit at Stage 2 is the set of reference instruments used in mapping. Each is named here briefly; cross-references point to the depth treatments in Part III.


#### The arc structures

Reference structures for typical hospitality arcs — full-service restaurant, fast-casual, bar, hotel, cafe — that serve as starting points for arc construction. The structures are starting points only. The actual arc for any engagement is constructed for the specific brand and customer, not adopted from the reference.


#### Peak-end logic

Drawn from the behavioural economics library (Part III, section 3.1.3). The principle that customer memory is disproportionately shaped by the peak and end of the experience. Used at the weight assignment move (2.2.2.4). Failure mode: peak-end applied mechanically as a rule, when in some engagements the brand may have strategic reasons to weight a different moment higher than the natural peak.


#### Moment-of-decision logic

Drawn from the behavioural economics library. Moments where the customer is making a decision concentrate cognitive load and emotional stake; these moments deserve weight and design attention. Used at the weight assignment move and again in Stage 3 where the design itself addresses the decision architecture.


#### Role-in-moment translation

The pattern for translating a Brand Role (Guide, Inspiration, Defender, Peer, Leader, Other) into role-in-moment for each specific point in the arc. The pattern is treated at the Brand placement move (2.2.2.5). Stage 1 establishes the Role; Stage 2 translates the Role into operational behaviour at each moment.


#### The eight expression dimensions

The brand's Personality Expression from Stage 1 supplies eight dimensions; the journey map identifies which dimension is dominant at each moment. This is the mechanism by which the brand book reaches the map and, through the map, reaches the design stage. The dimensions are: Physical Appearance, Dress and Style, Accessories and Distinctions, Language and Vocabulary, Tone of Voice, Volume and Sound, Attitudes, Beliefs.


#### Non-verbal communication for hospitality

Cross-reference to Part III, section 3.2. Particularly relevant at moments where the dominant expression dimension is Tone of Voice, Volume and Sound, or Attitudes — these dimensions are non-verbal channels through which the brand communicates. The non-verbal library is consulted in mapping to identify what specifically is communicated at these moments.


## 2.2.5 Patterns

This section holds the patterns the practice has accumulated at Stage 2. At version one, the section is seeded with universal patterns. It grows with every engagement.

Each pattern is presented in the standard form: when to use, what it produces, what it asks of the brand, failure mode.


#### Pattern S2-01 — The brand-specific arc

When to use: at the establish-the-arc move, when the practice or the founder is reaching for a generic hospitality arc.

What it produces: an arc that reflects this specific brand's customer experience, not a category-default arc. The discipline is to walk through the customer's actual path and name the phases as they occur for this brand.

What it asks of the brand: that it acknowledges where its arc differs from the category default. Often the divergence is at the start (a pre-arrival phase the brand owns) or at the end (a post-visit phase the brand owns).

Failure mode: an arc that is technically specific ("arrival" relabelled as "welcoming") but operationally identical to the category default. The test is whether the phases produce different moments, not whether they have different names.


#### Pattern S2-02 — The restrained HIGH count

When to use: at the weight assignment move, when the map is filling up with HIGH moments.

What it produces: a map where HIGH moments are a meaningful minority — typically three to seven, depending on map size. The restraint forces real hierarchy.

What it asks of the brand: that it accepts that not every moment can be a moment of truth. The brand's signature is in a few strong moments, not in unrelenting intensity.

Failure mode: a map where half the moments are HIGH, which produces a journey design that exhausts the customer and the staff.


#### Pattern S2-03 — The silent moment between peaks

When to use: when two HIGH-weight moments are placed close together in the arc and the customer has no space between them.

What it produces: a silent moment inserted between the peaks. The silence allows the first peak to be absorbed before the second arrives. The customer's experience of both peaks is amplified, not diminished, by the gap.

What it asks of the brand: that it resists the temptation to fill every gap with action. Hospitality brands default to presence; the silent moment is the brand's discipline of absence.

Failure mode: silence treated as a failure rather than a design choice. Staff trained without understanding the silence will fill it, undoing the design.


#### Pattern S2-04 — Role transposition at unexpected moments

When to use: when a moment seems too routine to require the brand's full identity — coat check, bathroom signage, the moment of placing a bottle of water — and the practice is tempted to leave the moment unmapped.

What it produces: the Brand Role explicitly transposed onto the routine moment, with the four placement fields completed. The routine moments are where brands most often go silent without meaning to.

What it asks of the brand: that it accepts that the brand is operating in every moment, not only in the moments the brand book anticipated.

Failure mode: routine moments left unmapped, then filled in Stage 3 with generic hospitality behaviour that quietly breaks the brand's coherence.


#### Pattern S2-05 — The reasoning note as discipline

When to use: at the close-the-map move, when the map appears complete and the temptation is to skip the Reasoning Note.

What it produces: a written explanation of every HIGH-weight placement, every silent moment, every brand-specific arc decision. Writing the Reasoning Note surfaces decisions that were made on intuition rather than on the brand book.

What it asks of the brand: an hour or two of writing at stage close. The Reasoning Note is short — typically four to eight pages — but it is the document that confirms the map is defensible.

Failure mode: a map without a Reasoning Note. The map travels into Stage 3 and partners begin to ask why decisions were made; without the note, the answers are reconstructed under pressure, and the reconstructions sometimes contradict each other.


## 2.2.6 Briefs generated

Stage 2 primarily produces an internal handoff — the journey map and the Reasoning Note are the brief that Stage 3 will design against. The map is not an external partner brief in the sense of the designer or architect briefs generated at Stage 1; it is the practice's instrument for organising its own subsequent work.

That said, Stage 2 may generate revisions to the briefs issued at Stage 1, where a journey decision constrains an architectural or design decision in a way the Stage 1 briefs did not anticipate. Two common cases.


#### Architect brief revisions

If the journey map identifies a phase that requires a spatial feature not specified in the architect brief — for example, a threshold moment that requires a particular arrival geometry, or a peak moment that requires a specific sightline — the architect brief is revised. The revision is versioned and dated, and the architect receives it formally rather than as a casual update.


#### Interior designer brief revisions

If the journey map identifies dominant sensory dimensions at specific moments — Volume and Sound at the bar phase, Physical Appearance at the threshold, Attitudes through the staff at the table — that the original interior designer brief treated generically, the brief is revised to make these dominances explicit. The interior designer can then make material, lighting, and acoustic decisions that support the brand at the specific moments where it matters most.

Stage 2 also produces the handoff briefing to Stage 3 — the internal document that tells the journey design stage what the map decided, why, and where the design has latitude versus where it is constrained. This briefing is part of the engagement archive and is treated in more depth at the harvest section.


## 2.2.7 Harvest

The harvest at Stage 2 is run at the close of the stage. It produces three outputs.


#### New patterns

Any pattern the engagement used that is not yet in section 2.2.5 is written up in the standard form. The threshold for recording a new pattern is one successful use; the threshold for promoting from candidate to confirmed is two.

Stage 2 is particularly likely to generate patterns of two kinds: arc patterns (specific phase structures that turned out to work for a particular kind of hospitality concept) and moment patterns (specific placements that recurred and worked). Both are recorded with the brand and concept context held abstract.


#### Template refinements

Any change to the journey map template suggested by the engagement is noted. Common refinements: a band that was always thin and can be removed, a band that was always crowded and should be split, a visual treatment of weight that did not read at a glance and should be revised.

Template refinements are made deliberately, in the version control discipline (Part V). A refinement is not made on the strength of one engagement unless the case is clear.


#### Library entries

Any new application of behavioural economics or non-verbal communication that emerged in mapping is added to the relevant library in Part III. Peak-end placement is the most commonly extended entry; each engagement produces a new context in which peak and end were located, and these contexts together accumulate into a working library of hospitality peak-end patterns.

The harvest is short — an hour or two at stage close.
