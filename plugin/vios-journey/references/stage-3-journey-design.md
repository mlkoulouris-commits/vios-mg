## 2.3 Stage 3 — Customer Journey Design


## 2.3.1 What this stage does

Stage 3 produces the design. The design is the customer's experience itself, specified moment by moment, in operational terms. Scripts, sensory cues, timing, choreography, the precise behaviour staff perform and the precise environment that surrounds the behaviour. This is where strategy becomes the room, and where the brand book becomes something the customer encounters.

Stage 3 is structurally different from Stages 1 and 2. The earlier stages produce strategic instruments — documents that hold the brand and the map. Stage 3 produces operational artefacts — design pages, one per moment, that read like specifications. The volume is larger: a complete Stage 3 deliverable for a single project can run to several hundred pages, because each HIGH-weight moment receives full design treatment and even MEDIUM and LOW moments receive standard treatments.

This is also the stage where the four converging inputs of the practice come together. The brand (from Stage 1) and the hospitality concept (which the engagement has worked alongside throughout) define what the experience is about. Behavioural economics (Part III, section 3.1) and non-verbal communication (Part III, section 3.2) define how the experience works on the customer — how decisions are shaped, how memory is formed, how presence is felt. All four inputs are present in every moment's design, in different proportions.

Stage 3 is the stage where the practice's method most strongly resists improvisation. The pressure to skip the design and let staff figure it out on the floor is constant — design pages are slow to produce, and an experienced operator can feel the choreography intuitively. The discipline of writing it down is that intuition does not survive turnover. Staff who joined six months in have no intuition; they have only what the design specifies. A brand that depended on the founder's intuition is a brand that disappears when the founder is not in the room.

The output of Stage 3 is the design document: one design page per moment in the journey map, organised by phase, with cross-references to the brand book and to the libraries in Part III. Alongside the design document is a small set of standing artefacts — the floor playbook, the staff reference materials — that are produced from the design and used in operation. These artefacts are introduced at the end of Stage 3 and developed in detail at Stage 4.


## 2.3.2 Method — designing the journey

The method has seven moves. The first is the intake from Stage 2. The second names the four converging inputs explicitly and the rule for how they combine. The third sets the working order — design begins with HIGH-weight moments, not with the first moment in the arc. The fourth is the design of a single moment, performed many times. The fifth connects the moments into the choreography that runs between them. The sixth designs the recovery — what happens when a moment goes wrong. The seventh closes the design with a coherence test.


### 2.3.2.1 Inputs from Stage 2

Stage 3 begins by reading the journey map and the Reasoning Note in full. The map names the moments and assigns weight; the Reasoning Note explains why. Both are read together; the map without the note is incomplete, because the map shows decisions and the note shows the reasoning that produced them.

Three artefacts from Stage 2 are held available throughout Stage 3:

The journey map. The map is the table of contents for Stage 3's work. Every moment on the map becomes a design page. Moments are addressed in the order set by the working-order discipline (2.3.2.3), not in the order they appear on the map.

The Reasoning Note. The note tells the design why each HIGH-weight moment is HIGH, why the silent moments are silent, and why the arc has the shape it does. Design decisions that contradict the Reasoning Note send the practice back to Stage 2, not forward in Stage 3.

The Stage 2 handoff briefing. This is the internal document produced at the close of Stage 2 that named where the design has latitude and where it is constrained. It is the practical companion to the Reasoning Note.

*Sufficient intake: all three artefacts read and held as working context. The intake is recorded in the project workspace as the opening of Stage 3.*


### 2.3.2.2 The four converging inputs

Stage 3 is the convergence point of the practice's four inputs. Each input contributes something distinct to every moment's design.


#### Input 1 — The brand

From the brand book: the Audience Persona, the Brand Role and Connection statement, the Archetypal Mix, the Personality Expression dimensions, the Differentiator, the Core Message Blocks, the Story Framework. The brand answers: who is this moment for, what role does the brand play here, what does the brand want the customer to feel or understand, what makes this moment ours rather than the category's.


#### Input 2 — The hospitality concept

From the operating concept: the menu, the beverage programme, the service model, the price point, the spatial reality, the equipment, the staffing levels, the operational constraints. The concept answers: what is operationally possible here, what does this menu require, what does this room allow, how does the kitchen actually plate.

The hospitality concept is not produced by VIOS; it comes from the chef, the operator, the founder. VIOS designs within it. A brand book that is incompatible with the concept has failed at Stage 1; a journey design that ignores the concept will not survive contact with the kitchen.


#### Input 3 — Behavioural economics

From the behavioural economics library (Part III, section 3.1): the principles documented in seventeen entries, plus the two foundational frameworks that organise them — System 1 and System 2 (the two modes of cognition) and the two selves (experiencing and remembering). The library answers: how does the customer actually decide here, which system is engaged in the decision, what is the cognitive load of this moment, what is the customer's reference point, how is the remembering self being formed, where can friction be removed and where must it be added.

Different moments draw on different principles. Moments dominated by System 1 — the threshold thin-slice, the felt warmth of the room, the snap judgement of the menu's typography — draw on cognitive ease, affect heuristic, thin-slicing, anchoring, and defaults. Moments where System 2 is engaged — choosing a wine, deciding on dessert, evaluating the bill — draw on choice architecture, framing, the System 2 mechanism of anchoring, and loss aversion. Moments that govern the remembering self — the peak, the end — draw on peak-end and duration neglect. The design names which framework and which principles apply at each moment, with cross-references to the relevant library entries.


#### Input 4 — Non-verbal communication

From the NV library (Part III, section 3.2): proxemics, paralanguage, kinesics, haptics, chronemics, environmental cues. NV answers: what is communicated without words at this moment, what is the geometry of the brand's presence, what does the brand's silence sound like, what does the room signal.

Most moments are dominated by non-verbal communication. The script is small; the proxemics, the tone, the timing, the orientation of the body, the use of silence, the surface textures the customer touches — these are most of what the customer experiences. The design names which NV principles apply at this moment, drawing on the library.


#### The convergence rule

All four inputs are present at every moment, but their proportions vary. A high-decision moment (ordering, choosing wine) is heavy on Brand, Concept, and BE; lighter on NV. A high-presence moment (the threshold, the farewell) is heavy on Brand and NV; lighter on Concept and BE. A high-operational moment (food delivery, bill settlement) is heavy on Concept, with Brand and NV providing the character and BE providing the design of the small decisions inside it.

The design page for each moment names the dominant inputs and references the relevant entries. The rule is that no moment is designed without all four inputs having been consulted; the rule is not that all four must be elaborated in every design page.

*Sufficient convergence: every design page references its dominant inputs and the relevant library entries. No moment is designed in isolation from the four-input frame.*


### 2.3.2.3 Working order — by weight, not by sequence

Stage 3 does not design the journey in the order the customer travels it. The temptation is to start at the threshold and work through to farewell. The discipline is to start with the HIGH-weight moments first, regardless of where they sit in the arc.

The reason is that HIGH-weight moments carry the brand most visibly and the design effort most heavily. Designing them first produces a centre of gravity for the design — a small set of moments that the rest of the journey orbits. The MEDIUM and LOW moments are then designed to connect to and support the HIGH moments, not to fill the arc.

The working order is therefore:

First pass — HIGH-weight moments. Each receives a full design treatment, with all relevant inputs elaborated. The peak and the end are designed first within this pass, because peak-end logic gives them disproportionate weight in customer memory and the rest of the design will be calibrated against them.

Second pass — MEDIUM-weight moments. Each receives a standard design treatment — shorter than HIGH, but full in all required fields. MEDIUM moments often draw their character from neighbouring HIGH moments and are designed in that context.

Third pass — LOW-weight moments. Each receives a compressed design treatment that names the action, the role, and the dominant expression dimension. LOW moments often fit a standard pattern (the standard food-delivery moment, the standard table-check moment) and may not require bespoke design at all.

Fourth pass — silent moments. The silent moments are designed last, but not skipped. The design of a silent moment is the specification of what staff do not do and where they are when they are not doing it. Silence requires a place to be silent from; the design says where.

*Sufficient working order: HIGH first (peak and end first within HIGH), then MEDIUM, then LOW, then silence. The order is documented in the project workspace as the design schedule.*


### 2.3.2.4 Designing a single moment

Each moment receives a design page produced against a standard template. The template's fields are not optional — every field is filled at each moment, even if some fields are short. The template is treated in detail at section 2.3.3; here, the method of completing a design page is described.

A design page is built in a single discipline: every field is answered by drawing on the four converging inputs, with the brand book as the centre of gravity. The questions the design page answers, in order:

What is the moment. A short name and a one-sentence description. Drawn from the journey map.

What is the customer's state. Where the customer is in the arc, what they are feeling, what they have just experienced, what they are about to experience. Drawn from the Audience Persona and from the Customer Target State field of the journey map.

What is the brand's intention. What the brand wants to happen at this moment. Drawn from the Brand Intention field of the journey map and from the brand book.

What is the action. What specifically is done — by staff, by the room, by the operational system — at this moment. This field is the heart of the design page. It is operational and concrete: the staff member approaches from this angle, says these words, places this object in this position. The action is named precisely enough that two different staff members trained on it would perform it the same way.

What is the script. Where the brand requires staff to use specific words, the script is written. Scripts are short — usually no more than one or two locked lines per moment — and they are exact. "Welcome" is not a script; "Welcome — sit anywhere" is a script. Scripts are written in the floor register of the brand's three-register language; if the floor register has not been established as part of the brand book, it is established here as a prerequisite.

What is the non-verbal frame. The body, the geometry, the silence, the tone, the volume, the timing. Drawn from the NV library and from the brand's Personality Expression dimensions. Most moments have more in the non-verbal frame than in the script.

What is the sensory frame. The environmental contribution — lighting, sound, scent, surface, temperature — that supports the moment. Some moments are sensory-dominant (the threshold moment is mostly sensory); some moments are sensory-neutral (the bill-arrival moment may have no specific sensory frame and inherits the room's standing condition).

What is the behavioural-economics design. What decisions the customer is making at this moment, and how the design supports or shapes those decisions. Drawn from the BE library. This field is often empty for low-decision moments and dense for high-decision moments.

What is the role of the room. What the physical space contributes at this moment. The room is always present, but at some moments the room is doing specific work — sightlines, acoustic separation, traffic flow, lighting register. At those moments the room's contribution is named.

What is the success metric. How the practice knows this moment worked. Stated as an observable signal, not as a sentiment. "The guest looked at ease" is not a metric; "The guest sat down without asking which table" is.

What is the failure mode. What it looks like when the moment fails — and what the recovery is. Brief at this point; the full recovery design is treated in the recovery move (2.3.2.6).

The design page may include additional fields for HIGH-weight moments: the brand-coherence test for this specific moment (does this design support the Brand Role and the Connection statement), the cross-reference to the Story Framework (where this moment sits in the seven-block narrative), the operational dependencies (what the kitchen, the bar, the host stand must support for this design to work).

*Sufficient design page: every required field filled, drawing on the four converging inputs, with cross-references to the brand book and to the relevant library entries. A staff member or a trainer who reads only the design page must be able to perform the moment without further context.*


### 2.3.2.5 Connecting moments — the choreography

Once individual moments are designed, the connections between them are designed. A journey is not a series of moments; it is a continuous experience, and the customer feels the joins.

Three kinds of connections are designed.

Transitions

The movement from one moment to the next. The walk from the threshold to the table. The pause between the last bite and the bill arriving. The interval between the order being taken and the food appearing. Transitions have their own design — pace, accompaniment, sensory continuity. A transition that is too long is a problem; a transition that is too short is also a problem. The design specifies the intended duration and the activity that fills it.

Handoffs

The points where one staff role passes the customer to another. The host hands off to the server; the server hands off to the runner; the bar hands back to the floor. Handoffs are sensitive moments — the customer can feel a clumsy handoff and read it as the brand losing track of them. The design names the handoff explicitly: who hands off to whom, what is communicated in the handoff (verbally and non-verbally), what the customer sees and does not see.

Continuities

The patterns that run through the whole journey and tie the moments together. The brand's tone, repeated across moments. The brand's geometry — how staff approach guests, regardless of which staff and which guests. The brand's silences — when the room is allowed to do the talking. Continuities are not designed at any single moment; they are designed across the whole journey and recorded in a separate continuity document that complements the moment-by-moment design pages.

*Sufficient choreography: transitions designed, handoffs named, continuities documented. The design now reads as a journey, not as a sequence of disconnected moments.*


### 2.3.2.6 Designing the recovery

Recovery design is the discipline of specifying what the brand does when a moment fails. Failure is treated as expected, not exceptional — every moment can fail, and a brand that does not have a recovery for the failures is a brand that handles them improvisationally, with results that vary by staff member and by night.

For every HIGH-weight moment, a recovery is designed. Common recovery scenarios are catalogued in the Patterns section (2.3.5). For each scenario the recovery design names: what triggers the recovery (the observable signal that the moment has failed), what the staff member does, what the staff member says, who is informed (if anyone other than the staff member needs to act), how the customer is left at the end of the recovery.

Recovery is held to a higher standard than first-pass execution. A moment that succeeds the first time is what the customer expected; a recovery that lands well is what the customer remembers. A brand whose recoveries are stronger than its first-pass moments is a brand the customer trusts more, not less.

Two failure modes in recovery design. The first is over-apology — recoveries that pile on apology to the point that they make the failure feel worse. The brand's character governs the recovery; a Quiet Refuser brand does not over-apologise, because over-apology is a behaviour the character refuses. The second is under-acknowledgement — recoveries that move past the failure too quickly, leaving the customer unsure whether the brand noticed. The design balances acknowledgement (so the customer knows the brand knows) with restraint (so the customer is not pinned to the failure).

*Sufficient recovery design: every HIGH-weight moment has a recovery; common failure scenarios are designed in pattern form; the brand's character governs the recovery's tone.*


### 2.3.2.7 Closing the design

The design is closed with a coherence test that runs across the whole document. The test asks six questions.

Does every moment on the journey map have a design page? If any are missing, the design is incomplete.

Does each design page draw on all four converging inputs, with the dominant inputs named and cross-referenced? If pages are missing inputs, those pages are revised.

Does the design support the Brand Role and the Connection statement at every moment, or are there moments where the role drifts? If the role drifts, the moments are revised or the brand book is reopened.

Does the choreography hold — transitions designed, handoffs specified, continuities documented? If any of the three is missing, the choreography work is completed.

Does every HIGH-weight moment have a recovery design? If not, the recoveries are completed.

Can a trainer who has not been part of the engagement read the design and train staff from it? If not, the design is too compressed or too referential.

The design is closed when all six tests pass. The handoff to Stage 4 begins here — the design becomes the source from which Stage 4 builds the training materials and the operating documents.


## 2.3.3 Template — the design document

The design document is the collection of design pages, organised in journey order even though they are produced in working order. The document has three parts: the design pages, the choreography document, and the recovery document. Each is built on its own template.


#### The design page

One page per moment, full A4 or letter, in landscape orientation. The page is divided into header, body, and footer.

The header carries: moment identifier (M-code from the journey map), phase, moment name, weight tier (HIGH/MEDIUM/LOW), and the peak/end marker if applicable. The header is consistent across all design pages so any page can be located quickly.

The body carries the design fields. For HIGH moments the body is the full set of fields described in section 2.3.2.4. For MEDIUM moments the body is a reduced set — moment, customer state, brand intention, action, script (if any), non-verbal frame, success metric. For LOW moments the body is a compressed set — moment, action, dominant expression dimension.

The footer carries: cross-references to the brand book sections that govern this moment, cross-references to the BE and NV library entries that apply, a note of any operational dependencies.

Visual treatment is uniform: same fonts, same spacing, same field positions across all pages. Uniformity is the discipline that makes the document usable as a working reference; designers who vary the layout to express the importance of certain moments produce a document that cannot be scanned quickly.


#### The choreography document

A separate document, shorter, that captures the transitions, handoffs, and continuities. The choreography document is organised by phase, with each phase containing the transitions within it and the handoffs at its edges. Continuities are listed at the front of the document — the patterns that run through the whole journey are read once and then assumed throughout the design pages.


#### The recovery document

A separate document that captures recovery designs by failure scenario, not by moment. Common failure scenarios — wrong order delivered, wait time exceeded, guest visibly unhappy, allergy disclosed late, table over-served, complaint at the bill — are each treated once and referenced from the design pages where they apply. This avoids repeating the same recovery design across every moment where it could occur.


#### On length

A complete Stage 3 deliverable is large. For a journey with thirty moments and seven HIGH-weight moments, the design document is typically two hundred to three hundred pages. The choreography document is twenty to forty pages. The recovery document is thirty to fifty pages.

The length is not a problem if the structure is right. The document is not read cover to cover. It is consulted moment by moment, in training (Stage 4) and in operation (after handoff). The discipline of consistent templates is what makes a long document usable rather than overwhelming.


## 2.3.4 Toolkit

The toolkit at Stage 3 is the largest of the practice, because the stage draws on the broadest set of instruments. Each is named here briefly with a cross-reference to its depth treatment.


#### The brand book

The full Stage 1 output is the working document at Stage 3. The Audience Persona, Brand Role, Connection statement, Archetypal Mix, Personality Expression dimensions, Differentiator, Positioning, Core Message Blocks, Story Framework, Brand Name and Tagline are all consulted in the course of designing moments. Cross-references in the design pages point back to the relevant brand book sections.


#### The journey map and Reasoning Note

The Stage 2 outputs are the table of contents for Stage 3 and the explanation for its weights and silences. Held available throughout the stage.


#### The behavioural economics library

Part III, section 3.1, treated in full depth. The two foundational frameworks — System 1 / System 2 and the two selves — establish the cognitive architecture. The seventeen principles in the library cover the practical mechanisms used at Stage 3, including: cognitive ease (the room's first read), thin-slicing (the threshold moment), anchoring (menu pricing, choice presentation), defaults (the choice that happens when nothing is chosen), choice architecture (the shape and order of options offered), framing (how the same thing is positioned), friction design (where to add or remove friction deliberately), social proof (how it operates in dining rooms), prospect theory and loss aversion (in offers and upgrades), peak-end and duration neglect (designed throughout, especially at the peak and end moments), and the affect heuristic (the room's emotional first signal). Each principle is cross-referenced from the design pages where it applies. The section 3.1.6 treatment of the limits of asking customers governs how customer feedback is used in design iteration.


#### The non-verbal communication library

Part III, section 3.2, treated in full depth. The dimensions most heavily used at Stage 3: proxemics (distance and zones for staff and guests), paralanguage (tone, pace, volume, silence as a tool), kinesics (orientation, eye behaviour, gesture economy), haptics (when and how touch is used or not), chronemics (timing, pace, the design of waits), environmental cues (lighting, sound, scent, surface texture). Each dimension is cross-referenced from the design pages where it dominates.


#### The three-register translation

Part III, section 3.3. The pattern by which concept-layer language becomes operational-layer instructions becomes floor-layer scripts. At Stage 3, the floor register is the dominant working register — scripts are written in it, and the operational register supports it from the design pages themselves. The three-register pattern is used at every moment where a script is written.


#### The choreography continuity patterns

A small set of standard patterns for how the brand maintains continuity across moments: the standard greeting geometry, the standard approach angle, the standard placing-objects-on-table gesture, the standard exit-from-table. These are patterns the brand may adopt or vary; the toolkit names them and the patterns section (2.3.5) records the variations the practice has used.


#### The recovery scenario catalogue

A standing catalogue of common failure scenarios in hospitality and the recovery patterns that have worked across multiple engagements. The catalogue is consulted at the recovery design move and added to as new scenarios emerge.


## 2.3.5 Patterns

This section holds patterns the practice has accumulated at Stage 3. Stage 3 is the most pattern-rich stage because moments recur across hospitality concepts even when the brand is different. The greeting moment, the seating moment, the menu presentation, the check-back — these appear in every engagement, and the practice's accumulated craft at each is itself a kind of methodology.

Each pattern is presented in the standard form: when to use, what it produces, what it asks of the brand, failure mode.


#### Pattern S3-01 — The locked greeting

When to use: at the threshold moment of any engagement where the brand's first impression must be specific and consistent.

What it produces: a single greeting line, locked in exact wording, delivered by every staff member who handles the threshold, in the same way every time. The locked greeting is the brand's first script and usually its most-repeated. Variations are not permitted; the discipline of the locked line is part of what makes it land.

What it asks of the brand: a line that is short (typically two to six words), unmistakably in the brand's voice, and survivable on the hundredth repetition of the night. Long greetings, clever greetings, and improvisational greetings all fail this test.

Failure mode: a greeting that varies by staff member. The customer who has been before reads variation as inconsistency and infers that the brand has not decided who it is.


#### Pattern S3-02 — The silent walk

When to use: at the seating moment, between the threshold and the table, where the staff member is leading the guest into the room.

What it produces: a deliberate silence during the walk. No commentary on the menu, no preview of the evening, no chatter. The walk is the room's moment to do the talking; the staff member's job is to deliver the guest into it and step back.

What it asks of the brand: that staff are trained to resist the hospitality default of filling silence with friendly content. Silence requires confidence; staff who feel they are failing the guest by not speaking will revert.

Failure mode: a walk that becomes a tour. The guest is now receiving information rather than encountering the room, and the room's first impression is filtered through the staff member's narration.


#### Pattern S3-03 — The non-visible information rule

When to use: at any seating or arrival moment where the staff member is tempted to communicate information the guest can see for themselves.

What it produces: a discipline of communicating only information the guest cannot read from the environment. "That's your table" when the table is obvious is wasted air. "The bar is open if you'd like a drink before you sit" when the bar is visible is condescending. The staff member communicates only what is non-visible: tonight's specials that are not on the menu, the dish that is no longer available, the recommendation that depends on what the kitchen has just received.

What it asks of the brand: trust in the guest's competence to perceive the room. Brands that over-communicate read as treating the guest as needing help to navigate.

Failure mode: staff who default to over-communication out of nervousness or training in other concepts. The rule must be taught explicitly and reinforced.


#### Pattern S3-04 — The decision anchor at ordering

When to use: at the ordering moment, where the guest is facing a menu and a set of choices that produces cognitive load.

What it produces: a single anchor offered by the staff member if the guest signals indecision — "if you're not sure, the [dish] is what most people choose tonight" or "if you're not sure, I'll pour you something." The anchor reduces the guest's load and offers a default that the brand stands behind.

What it asks of the brand: a clear position on what its anchor dish is, why it is the anchor, and the willingness to defend it as a recommendation rather than presenting it as one option among many.

Failure mode: an anchor that drifts — what staff recommend tonight is different from what they recommended last week, with no reason connected to the menu's actual change. Drift undermines the anchor's authority.


#### Pattern S3-05 — The peak placement test

When to use: at the design of the peak moment, when the design is being calibrated against the customer's emotional arc.

What it produces: an explicit test of whether the moment designed as the peak is actually the peak the customer will experience. The test asks: if the customer were told the evening ended here, would they feel the evening was complete? If yes, this is the peak. If no, the peak is elsewhere and the design is recalibrated.

What it asks of the brand: honesty about where the customer's experience actually peaks, which is sometimes not where the brand wants it to peak. A brand whose differentiator is the food and whose actual peak is the conversation at the table has to design with both in mind.

Failure mode: a peak placed by the brand's preference rather than by the customer's experience. Peak-end logic operates on what the customer remembers, not on what the brand wants remembered.


#### Pattern S3-06 — The bookended end

When to use: at the farewell moment, where the end of the journey is being designed.

What it produces: an end moment that echoes the threshold — same staff member if possible, same geometry of approach, same brevity of words. The journey opens plain and certain; the journey closes plain and certain. The match between threshold and farewell is what tells the customer the brand was consistent across the whole experience.

What it asks of the brand: that the farewell is treated with the same care as the threshold, even though the operational pressure to rush departure is high. The bookend is one of the brand's signature decisions and must not be sacrificed to table turnover.

Failure mode: a farewell that drifts because the operational priority shifted. The customer who received a strong threshold and a thin farewell will remember the inconsistency more than the strength of the threshold.


#### Pattern S3-07 — The recovery within character

When to use: at every recovery design, when the temptation is to default to the hospitality-standard recovery (effusive apology, immediate compensation).

What it produces: a recovery written in the brand's voice and character, not in the category's default. A Quiet Refuser brand recovers with restraint — acknowledged, taken back, replaced, no elaboration. A Caregiver brand recovers with warmth and personal attention. A Defender brand recovers by removing the problem from the customer's experience before the customer has to negotiate it. Each character has its own recovery; none of them is the category default.

What it asks of the brand: that the recovery is designed in advance from the character, not improvised in the moment from the staff member's instinct.

Failure mode: recoveries that drift to the category default under pressure. Staff who have not been trained on the character-specific recovery will default to what they have done in previous jobs.


#### Pattern S3-08 — The threshold thin-slice

When to use: at the design of the threshold moment, whenever the temptation is to treat the threshold as a brief transit moment that does not require dedicated design effort.

What it produces: a threshold moment designed across all six non-verbal categories simultaneously — proxemics (the geometry of the staff visible from outside, the host's position relative to the door), paralanguage (the locked greeting in the brand's exact tone), kinesics (the body language of staff and other guests visible at entry), haptics (the door handle, the first surface the customer touches), chronemics (the pace at which the customer is greeted; the deliberate brief pause before the host moves), and environmental cues (the room's first read on lighting, sound, scent, surface). The threshold is designed as a peak-weight moment regardless of its brevity, because the thin-slice formed in the first three seconds anchors every moment that follows.

What it asks of the brand: investment in cues the customer cannot articulate. The threshold scent that registers without dominating; the soundtrack volume calibrated to a specific decibel range; the staff posture that has been coached to a standard; the host stand's exact position relative to the door. Each of these costs effort to design and to maintain. The cost is justified because the thin-slice is real, automatic, and anchored — it will be made whether the brand has designed it or not.

Failure mode: designing only the conscious cues — the welcome message on the wall, the host's introduction script, the menu offered at the door. These engage the customer's System 2 awareness but do not address the System 1 thin-slice that has already formed. Design that addresses only what the customer notices is design that misses the actual mechanism.

Companion pattern reference: this pattern works in close companion with Pattern S3-01 (the locked greeting) and Pattern S3-02 (the silent walk). Together the three constitute the brand's threshold sequence — the customer thin-slices the room in three seconds; the host delivers the locked greeting; the silent walk lets the room continue its work. The three patterns are designed as one composition; coaching at Stage 4 addresses them together.


## 2.3.6 Briefs generated

Stage 3 generates operational briefs to the partners who execute the physical and operational components of the design. Two are common: the operations brief and the equipment/layout brief. The interior designer brief from Stage 1 may also be revised at this stage if the design surfaces requirements the original brief did not anticipate.


#### The operations brief

The operations brief specifies what the operating team must support for the journey design to work. Staffing levels by phase and by weight tier. Roles and responsibilities for the moments — who does the threshold, who handles the seating, who runs the check-back. Service flow constraints — how many tables a server can hold while still executing the design at the level it specifies. The brief is given to the operations partner or to the client's operations team.

The brief includes a section on what the design assumes the operations team will deliver, with these assumptions stated plainly. If the operations team cannot deliver an assumption, the design returns to the practice for revision. The relationship between design and operations is mutual: design constrains operations, and operations constrain design. Stage 3 reconciles the two before handoff.

Typical length: ten to twenty pages.


#### The equipment and layout brief

Where the design has specific requirements of equipment or layout — a particular host stand position, a specific bar geometry, a designated spot for plate pickup, a fixed location for the bill folder — these are documented in an equipment and layout brief. The brief is given to the operations partner or the operations team and, where relevant, returns to the architect or interior designer for execution before the operation opens.

The brief includes both the requirement (the specific equipment or layout feature) and the reason (the design moment it supports). The reason is essential — without it, the requirement reads as preference and may be quietly de-prioritised in budget or build.

Typical length: six to twelve pages, often with diagrams or photographs.


#### Revisions to Stage 1 briefs

Stage 3 may surface requirements that the Stage 1 briefs (architect, graphic designer, interior designer) did not anticipate. Common cases: the design requires a sightline the architect brief did not call out, a moment requires a graphic element (a wall chart, a signage piece) the graphic designer brief did not include, a sensory moment requires a material the interior designer brief did not specify. In each case, the brief is revised, versioned, and reissued to the partner.

Revisions are not failures of Stage 1. They are the practice's discipline of letting downstream stages surface upstream constraints. A practice that does not allow revisions ends up with briefs that are correct on paper and partners who deliver work that does not fit the engagement.


## 2.3.7 Harvest

The harvest at Stage 3 is run at the close of the stage. Because Stage 3 produces the largest volume of output in the practice, its harvest is correspondingly larger than the harvests of Stages 1 and 2.

The harvest produces four outputs.


#### New patterns

Any moment design that recurred and worked is recorded as a pattern in section 2.3.5. The threshold for recording is one successful use; the threshold for promoting to confirmed is two. Stage 3 is the practice's primary pattern factory — designed moments accumulate into a library of designs the practice can draw on in subsequent engagements without starting from blank.


#### Recovery scenario additions

Any new failure scenario encountered during the engagement — and the recovery designed for it — is added to the recovery scenario catalogue. The catalogue grows substantially across engagements and becomes one of the practice's most valuable accumulated assets, because failure scenarios are largely concept-independent and the recoveries travel.


#### Library entries

Stage 3 generates more applications of behavioural economics and non-verbal communication than any other stage. Each design page references library entries; many of those references represent new applications that should be added to the libraries themselves. Stage 3's harvest is the primary mechanism by which the libraries grow.


#### Template refinements

Any change to the design page template, the choreography document structure, or the recovery document structure suggested by the engagement is noted. Template refinements at Stage 3 are particularly consequential because the templates govern several hundred pages of output per engagement; a refinement that saves five minutes per page saves substantial time per project. Refinements are made deliberately, in the version control discipline (Part V).

The harvest at Stage 3 is longer than the harvests at Stages 1 and 2 — typically a half-day to a day, not an hour. The investment is worth making: the patterns harvested here are the most directly reusable assets the practice produces.
