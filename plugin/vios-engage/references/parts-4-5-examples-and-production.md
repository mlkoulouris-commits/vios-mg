# Part IV — Worked Examples

## 4.1  Why Part IV exists

Part IV is reserved for case studies — engagements where the practice's method was applied to a real brief, and the application taught the practice something worth preserving. At version one of the manuscript, Part IV contains only the format specification and a structural placeholder. Cases are added as engagements complete and as the practice decides that each case is ready to publish.

The function of Part IV in the manuscript is not portfolio. The brand book at Stage 1, the journey design at Stage 3, the training programme at Stage 4 — these are the practice's portfolio in their finished form, and they belong with the client. Part IV is the practice's record of what its method produced when applied to a specific brief, with the lessons extracted in the practice's voice rather than in the client's. The audience is the practice itself, plus future practitioners and collaborators who need to understand how the method behaves in real engagements.

This distinction matters. Part IV is not where the practice shows its work to prospective clients (that material lives in separate sales documents). Part IV is where the practice's accumulated craft is recorded in a form that can be consulted in future engagements. A new client whose situation resembles a prior worked example can be approached with the lessons of that example already available; the practice does not rediscover them from scratch.

Part IV is also a discipline. Writing a worked example forces the practice to articulate what it actually learned from an engagement, in writing, with distance. Engagements close at speed; the lessons are clearest when the work is fresh but often unwritable until enough time has passed for the practitioner to see them cleanly. The case study is the bridge between fresh experience and accumulated knowledge.

## 4.2  Format of a worked example

Each worked example follows a standard structure. The structure is uniform so that the practice's accumulated cases can be consulted as a single body of work, with the same questions answered in the same order across every case.

The structure has seven sections.

Section 1 — The brief

What the engagement was. The brand, the concept, the founder's starting position, the scope of the engagement (which of the four stages were in scope, which were not, what supporting work was included or excluded). The brief section is one to three pages and is descriptive — it sets the scene without commentary.

Section 2 — The brand strategy that emerged

A summary of the output of Stage 1: the Purpose, Vision, Mission, Values; the Audience Persona at the core layers; the Brand Role and Connection statement; the Brand Archetypal Mix; the Differentiator; the Positioning Statement. The summary is short — typically two to four pages — and presents the strategy as it was at the close of Stage 1. The strategy may have evolved through later stages; if it did, the evolution is noted but the original is preserved.

Section 3 — The journey map

A summary of the Stage 2 output: the arc, the moments-of-truth, the weight distribution, the silent moments, the peak and the end. Two to three pages. The map itself may be reproduced in summary form; the full map sits in the engagement archive.

Section 4 — The journey design

A summary of the Stage 3 output, organised by phase. The peak moment is treated in depth — its design, its rationale, its execution. The end moment is treated in depth. Three to five HIGH-weight moments are summarised. The recovery designs that the engagement produced are listed. Three to six pages.

Section 5 — The training programme

A summary of the Stage 4 output: the document architecture as applied, the cadence as applied, the coaching infrastructure as established. Specific innovations the engagement produced — a particular pre-shift format that worked, a workbook chapter structure that emerged, a coaching guide that became a pattern — are named. Two to four pages.

Section 6 — The lesson

This is the most important section of the case. The lesson section asks: what did this engagement teach the practice that the practice did not already know? The answer is specific. Not "this engagement taught us the importance of brand strategy" — that is generic. "This engagement taught us that a Defender brand serving a Caregiver audience produces a hybrid Role that requires its own design moves; the existing patterns for either Defender or Caregiver are insufficient when both are present." That is specific.

A case without a specific lesson is a case that should not have been written. The lesson section is what justifies the case's inclusion in Part IV. Without it, the case is a portfolio piece, not a contribution to the practice's accumulated knowledge.

The lesson section is the practice's voice, not the client's. It speaks to other practitioners — current or future — about what to take forward from this work. Length: one to three pages, often a single sustained argument.

Section 7 — What changed in the manuscript

The closing section names the specific changes to the manuscript that the engagement produced. New patterns added to which sections. New principles added to which libraries. Template refinements made to which Stage chapters. The list is brief but precise — a reader of the case who wants to find the changes in the manuscript can locate them.

This section is the bridge between the case and the practice's evolution. It also serves as a quality check on the case itself: if the engagement did not produce any changes to the manuscript, the case is probably not yet ready to be written; the lessons may not be specific enough.

## 4.3  The criterion for adding a case

Not every engagement becomes a Part IV case. The criterion is whether the engagement taught the practice something. The bar is deliberately high.

Two failure modes if the bar is lower.

If every engagement produces a case, Part IV becomes a portfolio rather than a body of craft. The cases blur together because most engagements teach the practice incrementally — small refinements, minor pattern variations — rather than substantively. A Part IV crowded with incremental cases is harder to consult than a Part IV with fewer, sharper cases.

If cases are added by virtue of project size or client prominence, Part IV becomes a sales asset rather than a practice document. The practice has separate sales material; Part IV serves a different purpose. A small engagement with a small client may produce a sharper lesson than a large engagement with a famous one, and the small engagement deserves the case slot more.

The decision to write a case is made at the engagement-level retrospective (Stage 4 harvest, section 2.4.7). The retrospective produces a recommendation; the decision to actually write the case is made later — typically three to six months after the engagement closes, when the lesson has had time to clarify and the practitioner can see the engagement with distance.

Cases that are recommended but never written are themselves a useful signal: the lessons that seemed valuable at retrospective often do not survive distance. A case that still seems worth writing six months out is more likely to teach something real than a case rushed to writing while the engagement is still hot.

## 4.4  Reserved structure

The remainder of Part IV is structural placeholder. Cases are added sequentially as they are written, each occupying its own chapter.

*[Worked Example 01 — to be added when the first case is written and ready. Recommended at engagement-level retrospective; final write-up scheduled when the lesson has clarified with distance.]*

*[Worked Example 02 — slot reserved.]*

*[Worked Example 03 — slot reserved.]*

Slots are added as needed. Numbering is sequential by date of writing, not by date of engagement; a case from an earlier engagement that is written later receives the higher number. The numbering preserves the order in which the practice came to articulate its lessons.

When Part IV reaches roughly six to ten cases, two things change. First, an index is added at the front of Part IV that allows consultation by lesson type (brand-character lessons, journey-design lessons, training-programme lessons, etc.) rather than by case number. Second, the question of whether the cases should spin out into their own document — alongside the pattern library spin-out described in Part V — comes up for consideration. Until then, the cases live inside the manuscript.

# Part V — The Production System

## 5.1  Why Part V exists

Part V is what makes the practice compound rather than repeat. The methodology, the pattern library, and the project workspaces relate to each other through specific protocols: how a new engagement begins, how a closing engagement contributes back, how the manuscript itself is versioned. Without these protocols, the methodology is a document that sits on a shelf. With them, the practice gets sharper with every project.

The four stages of the methodology describe how a single engagement is done. Part V describes how the practice — as an ongoing entity that does many engagements over time — operates. The two scopes are different. A practice that has only the four stages can produce excellent work on every engagement and still not improve from one to the next. A practice that has the four stages plus the production system improves continuously: the third engagement is sharper than the first, the fifth sharper than the third, the tenth substantially sharper than the first.

Compounding matters because the alternative — discovering everything fresh on every engagement — is what most consultancies do. The methodology exists in the consultant's head; it transfers to no one; when the consultant retires or leaves, it leaves with them. The practice that has a production system is a practice that exists as a body of accumulated craft independent of any single practitioner. This is what allows the practice to outgrow its founder.

Part V is written with the assumption that the practice may grow beyond a single practitioner within a foreseeable horizon. Many of the protocols here are useful even for a solo practice (the harvest discipline benefits the solo practitioner directly) but become essential when a second collaborator joins. The protocols are designed so a senior collaborator can be onboarded without rebuilding the entire system from the founder's memory.

## 5.2  The three layers in detail

The practice operates across three layers. Each layer has its own rhythm, its own contents, and its own rules. The layers were introduced briefly in Part I, section 1.7; this section treats them in detail.

Layer 1 — The methodology

Layer 1 is this manuscript. It contains the four-stage method, the two transversal libraries, the worked examples, and the production system. It is the theory of the practice — the universal layer that travels from project to project.

Layer 1 is stable. It is versioned. It changes deliberately, when a real learning forces an update, not opportunistically. Version increments are logged; the change log is itself part of the manuscript. Anyone joining the practice reads Layer 1 first and reads it in full; subsequent consultation is by section as the work requires.

Layer 1 is also disciplined in what it does not contain. Project-specific content does not enter Layer 1. Specific brand names, specific journey designs, specific training materials from any engagement belong in Layer 3 (project workspaces). Layer 1's discipline is to remain universal.

The single exception to this is Part IV — worked examples — which contains case-specific material. The exception is bounded: Part IV is the section where cases live, and the cases live there as instruments for teaching the universal method, not as records of the engagements themselves. The engagement records sit in Layer 3.

Layer 2 — The pattern library and reference cases

Layer 2 grows continuously. Every project contributes. Patterns harvested from engagements, library entries added to the transversal libraries, refined templates, accumulated worked examples — these are Layer 2's contents. At version one of the manuscript, Layer 2 lives inside Layer 1, in clearly demarcated sections (the Patterns section of each stage chapter, the principle entries in the BE and NV libraries, the worked examples in Part IV).

The expectation is that Layer 2 will outgrow its host and spin out into its own home. The condition for spin-out, and the migration protocol, are treated in section 5.6.

Layer 2's rhythm is the rhythm of engagements. Each engagement's harvest contributes to Layer 2; in steady state the practice adds to Layer 2 several times a year. The cumulative growth is significant. By the fifth engagement, Layer 2 typically contains thirty to fifty patterns across the stages, fifteen to twenty-five new library entries, four to six refined templates. By the tenth engagement, these numbers roughly double. The accumulated material represents the practice's accumulated craft and is the asset that compounds.

Layer 3 — Project workspaces

Layer 3 is the set of project workspaces, one per engagement. Each workspace is a dedicated space — typically a Claude project, a folder in shared storage, a document repository — that holds the engagement's working materials: the filled-in worksheets, the journey map and Reasoning Note, the design pages, the training materials, the briefs issued to partners, the correspondence with the client.

Layer 3 is the engagement's record. It exists separately from Layer 1 (the methodology) and Layer 2 (the patterns). The discipline is that what happens in Layer 3 stays in Layer 3, with one exception: at engagement close, the harvest extracts what is universal from the project workspace and contributes it to Layer 2 (and, where the case justifies, to Part IV of Layer 1).

Layer 3 is the layer most exposed to client confidentiality. Project materials may contain commercially sensitive information, founder-private reasoning, partner correspondence, financial data. Layer 3 is structured to respect these — separate workspace per project, access controls per project, retention policies per project. The harvest into Layer 2 is what extracts the universal lessons; the project-specific material remains in Layer 3 and is not surfaced elsewhere.

How the layers relate

The layers relate through specific transfers, run at engagement start and engagement close.

At engagement start (the intake protocol, section 5.3): material flows from Layer 1 and Layer 2 into Layer 3. The methodology informs the engagement's approach; the patterns inform the engagement's specific moves; the libraries inform the engagement's design depth. The new project workspace is populated with what the practice already knows.

At engagement close (the harvest protocol, section 5.4): material flows from Layer 3 into Layer 2 (and where justified, into Layer 1). New patterns are extracted; new library applications are added; template refinements are noted; if the engagement warrants a case, the case is recommended.

The layers do not flow into each other continuously — the flows are bounded by the intake and harvest protocols. This boundedness is what protects Layer 1's stability and Layer 3's confidentiality while allowing Layer 2 to grow.

## 5.3  The intake protocol

The intake protocol is the sequence of moves at the start of a new engagement. It runs from the first conversation with a prospective client through the formal opening of the project workspace. The protocol takes between half a day and two days of practitioner time, depending on the engagement's complexity, and is the engagement's foundation.

The intake has six moves.

Move 1 — The initial conversation

The practice meets the prospective client — usually the founder, sometimes with a partner or investor — to understand the brief. The conversation is structured around four questions:

☐  What are you trying to build, in your own words?

☐  What stage are you at, and what is the timeline?

☐  What partners, if any, are already engaged (architect, designer, chef)?

☐  What outcome would make you feel this engagement was worth the investment?

The conversation is not a sales pitch. It is the practice listening for whether the brief is one the methodology can serve. Some briefs are; some are not. A founder who wants only a brand book — without any interest in the journey design or the training — is a partial brief that the practice can take, with the scope adjusted accordingly. A founder who wants a full concept-to-operation engagement but is unwilling to slow down for Stage 1's interrogation is a brief the practice may decline, or may take with the explicit understanding that Stage 1 will not be rushed.

Move 2 — Scoping against the four stages

Once the brief is understood, it is scoped against the four-stage spine. Three questions:

☐  Which of the four stages are in scope, in full or in part?

☐  Which supporting briefs will be generated (architect, designer, interior, operations)?

☐  Which partners (the practice's roster or the client's) will execute the work the practice does not?

The scoping produces a one-page summary that names the stages in scope, the briefs to be generated, and the partner relationships. The summary is shared with the founder and ratified before the engagement begins. Scoping ambiguity at this point produces engagement drift later; the discipline is to over-name rather than under-name.

Move 3 — Pull from Layer 2

With the scope settled, the practice consults Layer 2 to identify what is already available. Patterns from prior engagements that may apply. Library entries with hospitality applications that match the new engagement's context. Templates that may be reused with light adaptation. Worked examples (Part IV) that resemble the new brief.

The pull is recorded as a starting set for the new engagement. The set is not binding — the engagement may produce reasons to deviate from the patterns or to generate new ones — but the starting set is the practice's accumulated craft as it bears on this specific brief. Without the pull, the engagement starts from blank; with it, the engagement starts from accumulated readiness.

Move 4 — Open the project workspace

A new Layer 3 workspace is opened for the engagement. Standard structure: one folder per stage, one folder for client-facing communications, one folder for partner briefs, one folder for the engagement archive (filled worksheets, journey map, design pages, training materials, harvest record). The workspace's structure is uniform across engagements so any practitioner moving between engagements knows where to find what.

The workspace's opening document is the intake record: the brief summary, the scope summary, the Layer 2 pull starting set, and the engagement timeline. The intake record is the workspace's table of contents and is updated as the engagement progresses.

Move 5 — Establish the engagement timeline

The engagement timeline is drafted at the close of intake. It names: when Stage 1 begins, when each stage's working sessions occur, when partner briefs are issued, when reviews happen, when the engagement closes. The timeline is approximate at this stage but is firm enough that the founder, partners, and the practice are working from the same expected pace.

The timeline includes the engagement-level retrospective — scheduled at the end of Stage 4 — and the case-writing window (three to six months after engagement close) where applicable. Naming these at the start ensures they are not forgotten when the engagement is heads-down in delivery.

Move 6 — Confirm and begin

The intake closes with a final confirmation from the founder: the scope, the timeline, the starting set, and the engagement's working rhythm are understood and accepted. The confirmation is documented; the engagement begins.

Engagements that do not pass through this intake — engagements that begin organically, sliding from a conversation into work without a formal intake — produce engagement drift that the practice then has to manage in delivery. The intake's small upfront cost prevents much larger downstream costs. The discipline is to run the intake even when the engagement appears to be small or the client appears to be known.

## 5.4  The harvest protocol

The harvest protocol is the sequence of moves at the close of an engagement. It runs from the formal close of Stage 4 through the final filing of the engagement archive. The protocol takes between one and three days of practitioner time, depending on the engagement's volume of output and the richness of the lessons.

The harvest has five moves. Most of them have been previewed in the individual stage harvests (2.1.7, 2.2.7, 2.3.7, 2.4.7); this protocol is the engagement-level harvest that integrates those individual harvests into a single contribution back to the practice.

Move 1 — Stage-level harvest review

Each of the four stage harvests is reviewed. The Stage 1 harvest produced new patterns for Stage 1, possibly template revisions, possibly library entries. Same for Stages 2, 3, 4. The harvest review consolidates: which patterns are confirmed across all stages, which template revisions hold across stages, which library entries add up to something larger than any single stage's contribution.

This integration step matters because some patterns the practice generates run across stages. A pattern that touches the brand book and the journey design and the training programme — a cross-stage discipline like the locked-greeting pattern (which begins as a brand voice decision, becomes a journey moment, becomes a script in training) — is more than the sum of its single-stage harvests. The engagement-level harvest recognises it as one pattern with reach across stages.

Move 2 — Library contribution review

The engagement's contributions to the two transversal libraries (BE and NV) are reviewed together. The libraries are referenced from every stage; an engagement typically produces three to eight new applications across the two libraries. The review consolidates these and writes the additions in the standard five-field form (Definition, Mechanism, Hospitality application, Worked illustration, Failure mode).

The contributions go into the libraries as new sub-entries under existing principles (a new application of anchoring, a new illustration of proxemics) or, occasionally, as entirely new principles where the engagement surfaced something the existing libraries did not name. The threshold for a new principle is high — usually two engagements have to surface the same gap before a principle is added — but new applications are added freely.

Move 3 — Template refinement review

Any refinements to templates — the brand book template (when defined), the journey map template, the design page template, the document architecture, the cadence calendar — that the engagement suggested are reviewed. Refinements proposed on the strength of a single engagement are usually held; refinements suggested by two or more engagements pointing in the same direction are made.

Refinements made at this point are logged. The change log identifies what changed, why, and which engagements informed the change. The log is part of the manuscript's version control (section 5.5).

Move 4 — Case recommendation

The engagement is assessed for Part IV case status. The criterion is whether the engagement taught the practice something specific. The recommendation is made now; the writing happens later, in the case-writing window (three to six months out).

A case-recommendation log sits in Layer 2 — a list of engagements that have been recommended, the proposed lesson, and the scheduled writing window. When the writing window arrives, the practice returns to the log, reviews the proposed lesson with distance, and decides whether to write the case. Many recommended cases are not written because the lesson does not survive distance; this is itself useful — the practice learns to recognise which kinds of lessons clarify and which dissipate.

Move 5 — Engagement archive

The full Layer 3 workspace is closed and archived. The archive includes: filled worksheets, the journey map and Reasoning Note, the design document, the training materials, all partner briefs and revisions, the client correspondence (subject to confidentiality terms), the intake record, and the harvest record. The archive is read-only after close; it serves as the engagement's permanent record.

The archive is not consulted in normal practice — once the harvest has extracted what is universal into Layer 2, the engagement-specific content is not reused. The archive's role is custodial: should a future client question, a partner dispute, a regulatory inquiry, or the practice's own retrospective need the original engagement materials, the archive provides them.

Engagement-level retrospective. The harvest closes with the retrospective: a short document, written by the practitioner, that names what worked, what did not, and what would be done differently in the next engagement. The retrospective is the highest-altitude part of the harvest and is sometimes the most useful — it identifies patterns of the practice's own behaviour that no single stage harvest captures.

## 5.5  Version control of the methodology

The methodology — this manuscript — is versioned. Version control is the discipline that allows the manuscript to evolve while remaining usable as a stable reference. Without versioning, the manuscript either drifts (changes accrete invisibly, and practitioners can no longer trust what it says) or freezes (changes are avoided to preserve stability, and the manuscript stops reflecting what the practice has learned). Version control allows both — evolution with stability.

The version-numbering scheme

The manuscript is versioned with a major.minor format. Major versions (1.0, 2.0, 3.0) reflect substantive structural changes — a new stage added, a major section reorganised, a fundamental shift in the practice's approach. Minor versions (1.1, 1.2, 1.3) reflect additions and refinements — new patterns added to stage chapters, new library entries, template refinements, new worked examples added to Part IV.

Most version increments are minor. Major version increments are rare and deliberate. At version one (the current state), no major increment is anticipated for at least the first dozen engagements; major changes require enough accumulated evidence that the existing structure is wrong, not just refinable.

What triggers a version increment

Three triggers.

First: a stage-harvest contribution that adds a new pattern, library entry, or template refinement. These are minor increments and happen at the rhythm of engagement closures.

Second: an engagement-level retrospective that surfaces a learning beyond any single stage. These are minor increments but more substantial — often a paragraph or section is added to a stage chapter or to a Part III library.

Third: an accumulated pressure across multiple engagements that requires a structural change. These are rare and are evaluated deliberately — the change is proposed, considered for at least one further engagement, and made only when the case is clear. A major increment is possible at this level.

Who can revise the manuscript

At version one, the practice is single-practitioner, and the practitioner has full revision authority. The discipline is to apply the same care to revisions as to the original writing — revisions are deliberate, are logged, are not made under operational pressure.

As the practice grows, revision authority becomes a question. The default expectation is that revision authority is held by the founder, plus any senior collaborator the founder explicitly grants the authority to. Junior collaborators may propose revisions but cannot make them directly. A proposed revision is documented (what is to change, why, which engagement informed it), considered by the authority holders, and either accepted (logged and made) or rejected (with reason).

The change log

The manuscript includes a change log at the back — a chronological record of every version increment, what changed, and the reasoning. Each entry: version number, date, summary of changes, the engagement or engagements that informed the changes.

The change log is the manuscript's history. It allows any reader, at any future point, to see how the practice's thinking evolved. It also serves as a check on revision discipline: a change log that grows quickly may indicate revisions are being made too easily; a change log that does not grow may indicate the practice is not learning.

The principle

Revisions are deliberate, not opportunistic. The pattern that worked once does not yet earn a place in the manuscript; the pattern that worked twice may. The library entry that surfaced once stays as a candidate; the entry that surfaced twice becomes part of the library. The discipline of waiting for the second instance is what keeps the manuscript trustworthy — readers can rely on what is in it because what is in it has been verified beyond a single engagement.

The exception is when a single engagement is unmistakably significant — an entirely novel concept, a brand-character combination the practice had never encountered, a failure mode that caused real damage. In those cases, the revision is made on the strength of one engagement, with the reasoning logged explicitly. The exception is rare and is itself a quality signal: a change log full of exceptions suggests the practice is not waiting for the second instance often enough.

## 5.6  When and how the pattern library spins out

At version one of the manuscript, the pattern library (Layer 2) lives inside the methodology document (Layer 1) — in the Patterns sections of the four stage chapters, in the principles of the two transversal libraries, in the worked examples of Part IV. This co-location was the right choice at version one because Layer 2 was empty: spinning out an empty library produces administrative overhead with no operational benefit.

The expectation is that Layer 2 will outgrow its host and require its own home. This section names when that happens and how to do it.

The condition for spin-out

The trigger is volume. When the patterns sections within the stage chapters, taken together, become longer than the chapters that contain them, the library has outgrown its host. At that point a reader of the stage chapter has to navigate past more patterns than method to find the method; the document has become a pattern library wearing a methodology jacket.

Quantitatively, the trigger is approximately when the Patterns sections of the four stage chapters together exceed seventy to ninety pages. The estimate is approximate and depends on the depth of patterns recorded, but the order of magnitude is reliable. This typically happens around the third or fourth engagement, depending on the richness of the engagements' harvests.

A second trigger, slower to arrive, is when Layer 2 becomes useful as a standalone consultation document. By the time Layer 2 contains thirty to fifty patterns and fifteen to twenty-five new library entries, practitioners begin to consult Layer 2 directly — searching for a pattern by type, rather than reaching the pattern through its stage chapter. When the standalone consultation use exceeds the embedded use, Layer 2 has become its own document in practice and should become its own document in form.

The migration protocol

Spin-out is a deliberate act. It has five moves.

Move 1 — Confirm the spin-out is needed

The triggers above are reviewed against the current state. If both are met (volume threshold reached, standalone consultation use established), the spin-out is justified. If only one is met, the decision waits.

Move 2 — Design the new home

The new home for Layer 2 is a separate manuscript or repository. Its structure is decided before any migration. Standard structure proposed: a Patterns section organised by stage (the four stage chapters' patterns become four chapters in the new home), a Libraries section that holds the accumulated BE and NV applications, a Cases section that holds the Part IV worked examples. The new home's structure is uniform with Layer 1 so practitioners moving between the two documents know where to find what.

Move 3 — Migrate

The patterns sections, library accumulations, and Part IV cases are moved from Layer 1 to the new home. The Layer 1 manuscript retains references — short pointers that say "consult the Pattern Library for the patterns at this stage" — but no longer contains the patterns themselves. The methodology document becomes lighter and is easier to read end-to-end; the pattern library becomes searchable and consultable on its own terms.

Move 4 — Update the cross-references

Every cross-reference in Layer 1 that pointed to a pattern, library entry, or case is updated to point to the new home. The practice's working documents and the project workspaces' intake records are also updated. The transition is hard to reverse once made, so the update is thorough.

Move 5 — Establish the new document's governance

The new home for Layer 2 needs its own version control. The discipline mirrors Layer 1's: deliberate revisions, change log, revision authority. The harvest protocol (section 5.4) is updated so engagement harvests contribute to Layer 2 in its new location.

After spin-out, the practice operates with two manuscripts and the project workspaces. The methodology is read by anyone joining the practice; the pattern library is consulted in the course of engagements; the project workspaces hold the engagement-specific work. Each document has its own rhythm; together they form the practice's complete operating system.

On not spinning out too early

The temptation to spin out is real, particularly when the practice is in growth mode and the additional document feels like a sign of seriousness. The discipline is to wait. Spinning out an empty Layer 2 produces overhead. Spinning out a Layer 2 that is still embryonic produces a document the practice does not yet consult, which atrophies and becomes a drag. The right time to spin out is when the document is so full it makes Layer 1 hard to use; before then, the co-location is correct.

## 5.7  Closing the production system

Part V closes the manuscript. The practice now has, in this document, the full theory: the four-stage method, the two transversal libraries, the patterns of translation and filter testing, the format of worked examples, and the production system that allows the whole to compound across engagements.

What the manuscript does not contain: any single engagement's specifics. The first worked example is yet to be written; the first patterns are yet to be harvested; the pattern library is empty in operational terms. The manuscript is theory; the practice is what happens when the theory meets a brief.

Three principles to close on.

First, the manuscript is for use, not for admiration. Every section was written to support a decision the practitioner is about to make on a project. If a section does not survive that test, it is rewritten or removed. The manuscript that sits on a shelf has failed; the manuscript that is consulted, marked up, returned to under pressure, is succeeding.

Second, the practice gets sharper with engagements, not with reading. The manuscript names what the practice does; the doing is where the craft is built. A practitioner who has read the manuscript carefully and run no engagements is not yet a practitioner. A practitioner who has run engagements without consulting the manuscript is not yet a practice. The manuscript and the engagements together produce the practice.

Third, the manuscript will be wrong in places, and the practice's job is to find where. The first version of any methodology contains errors that only emerge in use. The harvest discipline, the version control, the change log — all of these exist because the manuscript is expected to be revised. A practice that treats the manuscript as inerrant produces a methodology that ossifies; a practice that treats the manuscript as a working draft produces a methodology that improves. The aspiration is the second, not the first.

The manuscript ends here. The work begins, or continues, in the project workspace.
