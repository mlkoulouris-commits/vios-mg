## 2.1 Stage 1 — Brand Strategy &  Brand Book


## 2.1.1 What this stage does

Stage 1 produces the brand strategy and the brand book. The strategy is the substance — what the brand believes, who it is for, how it positions, how it sounds, what it stands against, the relational role it plays in the audience’s life. The brand book is the document that captures the strategy in a form that can be used downstream: by the practice itself in later stages, by the designer who builds the visual identity, by the architect who shapes the room, by the operations team that runs the floor, and by the founder who has to make decisions long after the engagement closes.

This is the most consequential stage in the practice. The work done here constrains everything that follows. A brand book whose strategy is weak produces a customer journey map that maps to nothing in particular, a journey design that is a series of stylistic choices without a centre, and a training programme that teaches staff to perform behaviours they do not understand. A brand book whose strategy is strong produces work that holds together for years and survives staff turnover, menu changes, and the founder’s absence.

The methodology is the full VIOS Branding Framework. It comprises a core sequence of fourteen interrogations, run in order, supported by a deeper reference layer that elaborates several of the interrogations with more refined working instruments. The core sequence and the reference layer are used together. Each interrogation has its own worksheet; each worksheet imposes the structure that makes the interrogation answerable. The interrogations are not independent — each one constrains the next, and each one can send the practice back to revise an earlier answer if a later interrogation surfaces a contradiction. The brand book is finished only when the fourteen interrogations resolve consistently.

Stage 1 takes longer than founders expect. The interrogation cannot be rushed, because rushed answers produce a brand that the founder agrees with in the room and then quietly drifts away from when execution starts. The discipline of the stage is to keep asking until the answer is one the founder will still recognise twelve months later under operational pressure.


## 2.1.2 Method — the brand strategy sequence

The method is fourteen interrogations, run in the order set out below. The order matters. The sequence begins with the founder’s intention — Purpose, Vision, Mission, Values — and turns outward to the audience and the competitive landscape. It then settles the brand’s point of difference, defines the brand’s personality and relational role to the audience, and produces the positioning statement. From there, the messaging architecture is constructed, the brand’s narrative is articulated, and the brand’s surface — name, tagline, designer brief — is finalised. Each interrogation deepens the brand and narrows the field of possibility.

At each step the founder’s answer is held against a single test: would the brand still recognise itself if this answer changed? If yes, the answer is provisional and the interrogation continues. If no, the answer has located something essential and is captured.


### 2.1.2.1 Brand Purpose

Purpose answers a single question: aside from commercial intent, why do we do what we do? The brand exists to make money — that is assumed. Purpose names the reason the brand would still exist if money were not the question.

The Purpose worksheet asks the question in five parts. Who or what are we making a commitment to. What are we going to do. How are we going to do it. What resources will we use. How often will we take action. The five-part structure forces purpose out of the abstract — out of the language of aspiration — and into a stated commitment with a recipient, an action, a method, a means, and a cadence.

A sufficient Purpose answer survives a specific test. The founder can describe the commitment to a stranger without invoking the commercial side of the business, and the stranger can repeat it back in their own words. If the commitment requires the commercial frame to make sense, it is not Purpose; it is a business plan.

Sufficient answer: five concrete responses across the worksheet, summarisable in a single sentence that does not require commercial framing to make sense.


### 2.1.2.2 Brand Vision

Vision answers what the brand aspires to be, where it aspires to go, and what it aspires to achieve. Vision is forward-facing. It names the brand at a future point and works backwards to the present.

Vision is distinct from Purpose. Purpose is the reason the brand acts; Vision is the future the action moves toward. A brand can have a clear Purpose and a vague Vision, in which case it acts consistently but without direction. A brand can have a clear Vision and a vague Purpose, in which case it moves toward something specific without knowing why. Both are weak. A complete brand has both, and they reinforce each other.

Vision is sufficient when stated specifically enough that the founder, twelve months in, can test a proposed decision against it and get a clear yes or no. “To be excellent” is not a Vision; it generates no test. “To be the place in our city that people return to for a particular kind of evening” is a Vision; it tests every proposed decision.

Sufficient answer: a stated future that produces a binary test against any present decision.


### 2.1.2.3 Brand Mission

Mission answers what the brand is committed to, in order to achieve its Vision. Mission is the bridge between Purpose (why we exist) and Vision (where we are going). It names the discipline the brand commits to in the present, in service of the future it has stated.

Mission is operational. It can be read as a set of standing commitments the brand will not break, regardless of pressure. Each clause of the Mission is a commitment that can be tested in operation.

Mission is the first interrogation that begins to constrain behaviour. Purpose and Vision are positions; Mission is action. A founder whose Purpose and Vision are clean can still arrive at Mission and discover they are not willing to commit to what their stated Purpose and Vision require. That discovery is the value of the interrogation. Better to discover it here than after the room is built.

Sufficient answer: a stated commitment, expressed as ongoing discipline, that can be tested in any operational decision.


### 2.1.2.4 Core Values

Core Values name what the brand holds dear in the way it does business. Each value is paired with a statement that translates the value into operating language.

The discipline of this interrogation is that values are not collected — they are reduced. Most founders, presented with a values worksheet, list eight to twelve. Eight to twelve values are not values; they are a list of qualities the founder admires. Real values are the three to five that the brand would defend against pressure, including pressure from itself. The interrogation continues until the list is short enough to be defended and specific enough to be tested.

Each value is written as a paired entry: the value itself (a single word or short phrase) and a statement that operationalises it. The statement is what makes the value usable. “Respect” as a value is vague; “We do not address guests with familiarity they have not invited” is operable. The values worksheet captures both.

Sufficient answer: three to five values, each with an operational statement, all of which would survive a moment where holding them costs something.


### 2.1.2.5 Audience Persona

Audience Persona is the longest of the fourteen interrogations. It runs across three layers — Demographics and Psychographics, Emotional Insights, Personality Insights — plus a Brand Affinity Audit that situates the audience in their existing brand world. The deeper Reference Tool elaborates the Personality Insights layer with an archetypal mix rather than a single archetype.


#### Layer 1 — Demographics and Psychographics

The first layer locates the audience in observable terms. Demographics: age, gender, occupation, education, personal income, household income, marital status, children, employees, location. Psychographics: car model, activities and interests, sports, music, values, clothing brands, social platforms, news sources, entertainment, publications.

The discipline here is specificity over generality. A persona whose age is “25 to 60” is not a persona; it is a market. A persona whose age is “38, with a five-year-old, lives in the second ring of the city” is a persona. The same applies to every field. The persona is given a name — invented, fixed, and used consistently across the engagement.


#### Layer 2 — Emotional Insights

The second layer names what the audience feels. Pain points: what is hard for them, in relation to the kind of experience the brand offers. Challenges: structural difficulties they face. Needs: what they require, often beneath what they say they want. Before-state emotions: how they feel before encountering the brand. Wants and desires: what they are looking for. After-state emotions: how they feel after the brand has delivered what it offers. Beliefs, captured as persona quotes — things this persona would actually say out loud about the category, the alternatives, themselves.

The emotional layer is where the brand begins to know its audience well enough to address them without explanation. A brand that can describe its persona’s before-state emotions accurately can build the journey design to move them deliberately to the after-state. A brand that cannot is guessing.


#### Layer 3 — Personality Insights (Archetypal Mix)

The third layer identifies the audience’s personality through the twelve-archetype framework: Outlaw, Magician, Hero, Lover, Jester, Everyman, Caregiver, Ruler, Creator, Innocent, Sage, Explorer. Each archetype has a drive, desires, fears, and characteristics.

The Reference Tool refines this layer by capturing the audience as an Archetypal Mix rather than a single archetype: a Core Archetype representing at least 70 percent of the persona, and an Influencer Archetype representing 30 percent or less. The mix is allocated across two archetypes, no more. The discipline of restricting to two prevents the persona from becoming a composite of everything; the 70/30 split forces a primary identity.

The archetype is not a label. It is a working hypothesis about what motivates this persona at a level deeper than demographics. It informs every later step. The brand’s own archetype, interrogated later, must be one that this audience archetype responds to. A Sage audience does not respond to a Jester brand the same way it responds to a Sage brand.


#### Layer 4 — Brand Affinity Audit

The Reference Tool adds a fourth layer: an audit of brands the persona already loves, captured on its own worksheet. For each brand, four fields: brand name, years used, alternative brands they consider, and the reason they like this brand aside from features and benefits.

The last field is the most important and the hardest to get honest answers on. Features and benefits are surface explanations; the reason behind the loyalty is usually relational, identity-based, or aesthetic. Capturing the real reasons across five or ten brands surfaces the patterns of attachment the persona forms — patterns the brand under construction will need to engage with.

Sufficient answer: a named, specific persona at all four layers, including a Core/Influencer Archetypal Mix and a brand affinity audit of at least five brands.


### 2.1.2.6 Competitor Audit

Competitor Audit is delivered as a separate working spreadsheet — distinct from the worksheet stack — that audits five direct competitors across six dimensions: Brand Message (tagline, slogan, underlying core message), Positioning (stated difference, value proposition, uniqueness, defensibility, communication of position, features and benefits highlighted), Personality (defined archetype, perceived personality, target appeal, voice and language, visual execution), Brand Identity (logos, colours, images, typography, packaging), Website and UX (design, mobile design, photography style, content, contact, support), and Reviews (what customers actually say).

Each competitor is audited on a sheet and scored. The scoring is not the point — the noticing is. The discipline is to look at each competitor with enough attention to see what they actually do, not what the practice already assumes they do. Direct competitors are usually closer in posture than the founder expects, which is itself a finding.

The audit closes with two synthesis sheets: Ideas (patterns observed across competitors that may inform the brand) and Opportunities (gaps observed across competitors that the brand may credibly fill). The Opportunities sheet feeds directly into the Differentiator interrogation that follows.

Sufficient answer: five competitors audited with specificity, plus a synthesised list of opportunities the brand can credibly occupy.


### 2.1.2.7 Differentiator

Differentiator answers a single question: what is the one thing we do differently from our competitors, that adds value to our audience and enhances their experience?

Three constraints make this question harder than it sounds. The differentiator must be one thing, not a list. It must add value to the audience as the audience would describe it, not as the brand wishes the audience would describe it. And it must enhance the experience — meaning it must show up in what the audience actually encounters, not in what the brand says about itself.

The worksheet captures the differentiator in two forms: Experience Keywords (the words that describe what the audience experiences differently) and the Differentiator Statement (what we do differently, and what that means to the audience). The two are paired. The keywords feed forward into the language of later messaging and into the keyword extraction at the brand name stage; the statement feeds forward into the positioning that follows.

Sufficient answer: a single stated point of difference, evidenced in what the audience experiences, supported by a short list of experience keywords.


### 2.1.2.8 Brand Personality

Brand Personality is the most elaborate interrogation in the sequence. The Reference Tool expands it across five distinct working instruments: the audience link, the Brand Role and Connection statement, the brand’s Archetypal Mix, the Brand Personality Expression, and the Brand Identity Notes. A sixth instrument, Persona in Practice, turns the result into rules every carrier of the brand can apply. Instrument 6 is provisional at version 1.3 (see below).


#### Instrument 1 — The audience link

The Brand Personality Workbook begins by re-grounding in the audience persona established at step 2.1.2.5. The brand’s personality is being interrogated for one purpose: to construct a brand that the audience responds to. The workbook displays the audience’s archetype, drive, desires, fears, characteristics, before-state and after-state emotions as the running context. Every personality decision is calibrated against the audience displayed in this view.

Two additional fields are captured at this step: Situational Desires (what the audience wants in their current circumstance, which the brand may respond to) and Personality Desires (what the audience wants the brand to be relationally, expressed in completion-form sentences such as “I can guide you,” “You are the same as me,” “I can protect you,” “We can be great”). The Personality Desires field directly informs the Brand Role selection that follows.


#### Instrument 2 — Brand Role and Connection

The Brand Role names the relational position the brand occupies in the audience’s life. The Reference Tool offers a defined set of options: Guide, Inspiration, Defender, Peer, Leader, Other. The brand selects one. The selection is binding for all downstream work.

Each Brand Role has a different relational geometry. A Guide is alongside the audience but ahead of them, pointing the way. An Inspiration is above the audience, modelling possibility. A Defender is in front of the audience, taking the threat. A Peer is beside the audience, sharing the experience. A Leader is in front of the audience, setting direction. The geometry shapes everything about how the brand presents, speaks, designs the room, and trains staff.

Paired with the Brand Role is a Connection statement — a sentence-completion exercise that translates the role into a relational claim. Examples: “I can guide you to the answer” for a Guide brand; “You are the same as me — we belong together” for a Peer brand; “I will protect you from what is wrong with the category” for a Defender brand. The Connection statement is the relational thesis of the brand.


#### Instrument 3 — Brand Archetypal Mix

The brand’s archetype is selected from the same twelve as the audience persona: Outlaw, Magician, Hero, Lover, Jester, Everyman, Caregiver, Ruler, Creator, Innocent, Sage, Explorer. The Reference Tool requires an Archetypal Mix: a Core Archetype representing at least 70 percent, paired with an Influencer Archetype representing 30 percent or less. Characteristics are captured separately for each: Core Characteristics on one side, Influencer Characteristics on the other. The two sets are not merged. The brand presents as the Core; the Influencer adds texture without diluting identity.

The archetype is calibrated against the audience archetype established earlier. The brand and the audience need not share an archetype — most often they do not — but the brand’s archetype must produce a relationship the audience archetype responds to. The Brand Role selected in Instrument 2 helps anchor this calibration: a Guide brand to a Hero audience is a natural pairing; a Peer brand to an Outlaw audience is a different but equally valid pairing; a Caregiver brand to a Ruler audience is improbable and should be questioned if proposed.

Once the mix is selected, the brand’s Drive, Desires, Fears, and Characteristics are captured — informed by the Core archetype, modulated by the Influencer. These four fields are the brand’s interior life. They are not communicated to the audience directly; they govern the brand’s decisions and behaviour.


#### Instrument 4 — Brand Personality Expression

Personality Expression is the brand’s outward presentation. Eight dimensions are captured, each in concrete terms: Physical Appearance (what the brand looks like, made tangible — usually anthropomorphised: if the brand were a person, what would they look like). Dress and Style. Accessories and Distinctions. Language and Vocabulary. Tone of Voice. Volume and Sound. Attitudes. Beliefs.

This is the section where the brand stops being abstract and starts being something a designer, a manager, or a staff member could recognise and reproduce. A brand whose Personality Expression reads as a paragraph of adjectives is incomplete. A brand whose Personality Expression reads as a description of a specific imagined person — with clothes, mannerisms, vocabulary, things they would never say, things they would always notice — is sufficient.


#### Instrument 5 — Brand Identity Notes

The Reference Tool captures initial Brand Identity Notes at the strategy stage, before the designer is engaged. Three fields: Colour Palette (the colours the brand gravitates toward, with reasoning rooted in the personality and the brand identity, not in trend), Typography (the typographic register the brand belongs to — geometric, humanist, traditional, vernacular — with reasoning), Image Style (the photographic and illustrative register the brand operates in — what is shown, how it is framed, what is refused).

These notes are not visual identity. They are direction. The designer engaged at step 2.1.2.14 receives them as part of the strategic brief and reaches their own visual solution within them. The notes prevent two failure modes: a designer who arrives without strategic constraint and produces work that looks generic, and a designer who arrives with strategic constraint but no direction and produces work that misreads the brand’s register.

A separate visual reference set is collected alongside the Brand Identity Notes: images that bring the personality to life. These are inspiration material, not specification. The designer will use them; they are not the brief.


#### Instrument 6 — Persona in Practice (provisional, added at version 1.3)

Instruments 1 to 5 establish who the brand is. Instrument 6 turns that person into rules that every carrier of the brand — staff, writers, partners, and AI tools — applies the same way, every time. It adds no new strategy. Every field traces back to an earlier instrument or step, and where the founder has not supplied an answer the field is marked “to confirm”, never invented.

The instrument is built in nine layers and recorded in the Persona in Practice template (section 2.1.4).


#### Layer 1, Persona core. The brand as one specific person: a one-line persona sentence (“We are the [role-person] who [relationship to the guest]”), a short portrait with habits and limits, the relationship geometry taken from the Brand Role, and the inner drive taken from the Archetypal Mix. The layer also names the carrier model. In a unified model one real person embodies every facet of the persona; in a distributed model several people each carry facets. The carriers are named by role.


#### Layer 2, Traits. Three to five traits, each with what it means in behaviour and what it must never become. The “never” is the overshoot this particular brand is tempted by — warm becoming gushing, knowing becoming lecturing — not a generic fault. Each trait traces to the Core or Influencer characteristics, the Personality Expression, or the Core Values.


#### Layer 3, Tone sliders. The brand’s home position and permitted range on five scales: serious to playful, formal to relaxed, functional to emotive, arm’s length to personal, direct to conversational. Voice stays fixed; tone moves inside the range.


#### Layer 4, Moment and touchpoint map. The slider positions and lead trait for each journey moment and channel, with the carrier who delivers it. The layer is drafted at Stage 1 and confirmed against the Stage 2 arc, where the dominant expression dimension per moment is set.


#### Layer 5, Do and don’t examples. At least two on-brand and one off-brand sentence per trait, and one pair per high-weight moment. The off-brand line must be a tempting mistake, not simply bad writing. People and AI tools learn the persona from demonstration far more reliably than from description.


#### Layer 6, Word rules. The brand’s own words (experience keywords, the audience’s own words), fixed house terms, words to avoid, words never used, and writing mechanics: sentence length, punctuation, address form, languages.


#### Layer 7, Hard moments. How the persona behaves when things go wrong: complaint, the brand’s own mistake, bad news, negative public review, saying no. For each, the first act, what is said, what is never done, and when a real person takes over. In hard moments the persona’s warmth stays but its display goes down and clarity goes up.


#### Layer 8, Final checklist. Ten lines or fewer that every piece of content passes before release, whoever or whatever wrote it.


#### Layer 9, Operational link. The bridge to Stages 2 to 4: each trait must show in at least one behaviour in the room, not only in copy. The layer is opened at Stage 1 and completed as the map, design, and training stages proceed.

The nine layers are compressed into an AI Persona Brief: a single instruction set that an AI tool loads for content creation or guest interaction. The brief is tested on three real tasks — typically a social post, a booking reply, and a complaint reply — and the founder marks each line on-brand or off-brand. Errors are fixed in the layer they came from, not only in the output.

Where AI carries the brand in guest interaction, four rules apply. The guest is told at first contact that they are speaking with an AI; in the European Union this is a legal requirement from August 2026, and the exact obligations are confirmed with counsel. The AI is a named assistant of the brand, never a person pretending to be staff. Humanlike warmth is reduced when the guest is frustrated. A real person is always one step away, and the AI says so. Where AI produces content, a named person reviews every piece before release and holds responsibility for it, and a periodic drift check samples AI output against Layer 8.

Sufficient answer for Instrument 6: a portrait a new staff member could recognise; named carriers; three to five traits with means and never-means; five sliders with home, range, and reason; a moment map covering every high-weight moment; on-brand and off-brand examples per trait; word rules usable as a search over a draft; all hard moments filled with a hand-over route; a checklist of ten lines or fewer; an AI Persona Brief tested on three real tasks.

Failure mode: the persona lives in the brand book and the marketing, and the people and tools that actually meet the guest have never met it.

Sufficient answer: audience link populated; Brand Role and Connection statement settled; brand Archetypal Mix with separated Core and Influencer Characteristics; eight Personality Expression dimensions in concrete terms; Brand Identity Notes in three fields with reasoning; visual reference set collected; Persona in Practice completed to its own sufficient answer, with Layer 9 left open for Stages 2 to 4.


### 2.1.2.9 Brand Positioning Statement

Brand Positioning Statement uses a five-part formula:

WE HELP [target audience]

WHO [pain-points]

TO ACHIEVE / [key benefit]

EXPERIENCE

UNLIKE [competitive alternative]

OUR SOLUTION [differentiator statement]

The formula is not a slogan and is never used as marketing copy. It is an internal positioning instrument that holds the brand’s place in the market in a single structured statement. The five parts pull from the prior interrogations: target audience from Audience Persona, pain-points from the Emotional Insights layer, key benefit from the audience’s stated wants and the brand’s offering, competitive alternative from the Competitor Audit, differentiator from the Differentiator interrogation.

The full statement is then written out as a single paragraph. Reading the paragraph aloud surfaces tension between the parts: if the differentiator does not solve the pain-point for that target audience, the positioning is not coherent. The interrogation is repeated until the paragraph reads as a single coherent claim.

Sufficient answer: the five-part formula filled in, plus a full-statement paragraph that reads as one coherent claim about the brand’s market position.


### 2.1.2.10 Core Message Blocks

Core Message Blocks structure the brand’s messaging into two tiers.

The Primary Core is composed of five message blocks: Audience (who they are and what makes them recognise themselves in the description), Pain-Points (what pain they experience in relation to the brand’s offer, and how that pain affects their lives), Key Benefit (what they will experience from the brand’s solution, and how those benefits impact their lives), Alternative (the closest competitive group, where they fall short, ideally the opposite of the differentiator), and Differentiator (the main point of difference and how it impacts the customer’s experience).

The Secondary Core is composed of four message blocks drawn from the foundational interrogations: Purpose, Vision, Mission, Values. Each is rewritten as a key message — not as a fact about the brand but as something the audience should take away.

Each block is written as a statement, not as copy. The statement is the strategic instruction for what any copy should communicate in that block — not the words themselves. Copy is downstream, produced by writers working from the statements. The blocks are the message architecture.

Sufficient answer: nine message-block statements, each one a single sentence that captures what the audience should understand. None of them is finished copy.


### 2.1.2.11 Story Framework

Story Framework structures the brand’s narrative across seven story blocks. The framework is hero-led: the audience is the hero of the story; the brand is the guide who helps the hero arrive at their desired outcome. This frame is consistent with the Brand Role established at step 2.1.2.8 — even a brand whose Role is not literally Guide still operates within the hero-led narrative, since the customer is always the hero of their own life.

The seven blocks: (1) The Existing World — who the hero is, where they live, what their life looks like before the story begins. (2) The Obstacle — life is not perfect; the hero has wants or needs that cause conflict. (3) The Call to Action — something happens that sparks the hero into action, with the consequences of inaction made vivid. (4) Meeting the Guide — the brand appears, calms the hero’s mind, demonstrates credibility, and reassures the hero of the direction to the desired outcome. (5) The Challenge — the hero opens up their vulnerability; the emotional consequence beneath the practical problem is surfaced. (6) The Transformation — the payoff; the contrast between the before-state and after-state allows the audience to feel the desire for resolution. (7) The New World — the hero’s life without the problem; what their life looks like with the resolution in place.

Each block is written as a short story, in specific terms. The framework is then the foundation for all microstories — every piece of brand storytelling that follows draws from the framework. Campaigns, copy, social posts, founder interviews — all of them are micro-instances of the seven-block frame.

Sufficient answer: seven story blocks, each a short, specific narrative; together they cohere as a single arc.


### 2.1.2.12 Brand Interrogation &  Brand Name

Brand Name is reached only after the strategy is settled. The Reference Tool surrounds the naming work with a deep interrogation — a set of questions designed to surface the brand’s character at a level of specificity that produces both naming candidates and refinements to everything established earlier.

The deep interrogation asks the brand:

- What do you love and why?
- What do you dislike, or hate, and why?
- Where do you bring the most value to your audience?
- What is the one thing you would change about your industry above all else, and why?
- Why is your market a great space to be in?
- What positives and negatives do you believe social media has brought about in our society?
- What is the purpose of your existence?
- What is important to you in the way you do business?
- What does your audience need to be protected against?
- What do you believe are the most pressing issues in the world today?
- What are you passionate about?
The questions are answered at length, not in single phrases. The answers are then mined for keywords — words, phrases, and ideas that recur across the answers and that the brand keeps returning to. The keywords are the raw material for name candidates.

The naming worksheet captures keywords in one column and candidate names in the other. Candidates are generated using brand name development techniques: literal extraction (a keyword used directly), morphology (modifications of a keyword), combination (two keywords joined), metaphor (a keyword that stands for the brand), abstraction (a coined word with the keyword’s sonic or rhythmic quality).

The candidate names are not judged in isolation. They are tested against the strategy built in the prior interrogations: archetype, role, positioning, personality expression, audience. A name that is well-formed but tests poorly against the brand’s strategy is set aside.

The interrogation produces a long list and a short list, in that order. The short list is reduced through additional tests: pronounceability in the local language and in any languages the brand may operate in, ownership availability (domain, trademark, social handles, but only after a strategic shortlist is built), distinctiveness from competitors audited at step 2.1.2.6, and the test of repetition — does the name still hold up after being said fifty times in fifty different operational contexts.

Sufficient answer: the deep interrogation answered at length, a keyword set extracted, a long list of name candidates generated, a short list tested against strategy, and a chosen name documented with the reasoning behind the choice.


### 2.1.2.13 Tagline

Tagline follows the same keyword-driven process as Brand Name, applied to the question of the brand’s short-form expression. The worksheet captures keywords and tagline concepts. The tagline is tested against the same constraints as the name, plus one more: the tagline must survive being placed next to the brand name and read as one thing, not two unrelated phrases.

A tagline is not required. Many brands operate without one. The interrogation is therefore not a forced choice. If the tagline does not emerge with the conviction the rest of the strategy carries, it is not adopted. A weak tagline harms the brand more than no tagline.

Sufficient answer: either a tagline that earns its place next to the brand name, or a documented decision not to adopt a tagline at this stage.


### 2.1.2.14 Designer Selection

Designer Selection closes the brand strategy stage. The Designer Checklist is applied to any designer being considered for the visual identity work. Six criteria:

Processes. The designer follows a strategic structure, evidenced by what they ask of the brand before they begin design work. A designer who starts with design without strategic intake is not the right designer.

Language. The designer speaks the brand’s working language as a native, or — if foreign — can summarise the brief back in their own words clearly enough that the practice can verify they have understood it.

Variation. The designer’s portfolio shows work across multiple industries and stylistic registers. A designer with a single style across all work is not strategically adaptable.

Style Guide. The designer commits to delivering a style guide as part of the engagement. A designer who does not produce style guides produces visual identity that drifts.

Interview. The designer is interviewed over a video call. The brand confirms it can speak openly with the designer at any stage of the engagement, including stages where the work is difficult.

The Brief. The designer receives all relevant strategic information from this stage — the full brand book, including the Brand Identity Notes from step 2.1.2.8 — and commits to designing the visual identity with that information at the centre. Preferably, the designer takes the lead in extracting any additional information they need.

Sufficient answer: a designer chosen against all six criteria, with the brief ready to issue.


#### Closing the sequence

The fourteen interrogations together produce the brand strategy. The brand book is the document that captures the output. The brand book format is treated separately in section 2.1.3.

The strategy is not finished when the worksheets are filled. It is finished when the whole sequence reads as one coherent thing. The closing test: read the strategy in sequence, from Purpose through Designer Selection. Does each step follow naturally from the prior step? Does the differentiator solve the pain-point named in Audience Persona? Does the Brand Role respond to the audience’s Personality Desires? Does the brand’s Archetypal Mix produce a relationship the audience’s Archetypal Mix accepts? Does the positioning statement use the differentiator as its solution? Does the story framework cast the audience as a hero this brand can credibly guide? If any check fails, the relevant step is revisited. The brand is not closed until every check passes.

The brand book is a working document, not a decorative one. It is read by VIOS in the course of the engagement, by the partners who receive briefs derived from it, and by the founder and the operating team after the practice has handed off.

## 2.1.4 Toolkit

The toolkit at Stage 1 is the set of reference instruments used in the course of the fourteen interrogations. Each is named here briefly; cross-references point to the depth treatments in Part III.


#### The Branding Framework

The full Branding Framework is the methodology’s primary instrument. It comprises the fourteen worksheets of the Complete Branding Course and the Reference Tool that elaborates several interrogations with deeper working instruments (the audience archetypal mix, the Brand Role and Connection statement, the brand archetypal mix with separated characteristics, the Brand Identity Notes, the Brand Affinity Audit, the deep brand interrogation). The framework is not optional, not summarised, and not skipped. The worksheets are filed at the close of the stage as the engagement archive.


#### The twelve-archetype framework

The twelve archetypes — Outlaw, Magician, Hero, Lover, Jester, Everyman, Caregiver, Ruler, Creator, Innocent, Sage, Explorer — provide the personality framework for both the audience persona and the brand. The framework is used at two points: Audience Persona (Personality Insights) and Brand Personality. The Reference Tool requires an Archetypal Mix at both points: a Core Archetype at 70 percent or more, paired with an Influencer Archetype at 30 percent or less, with characteristics captured separately for each. Supporting references — the Archetypes Reference Tool, the Brand Personality Questionnaire, the Brand Personality Workbook, Audience Persona Branding with Personality, and Characteristics and Traits — sit alongside as deeper material.


#### The Brand Role typology

Six relational positions the brand may occupy in the audience’s life: Guide, Inspiration, Defender, Peer, Leader, Other. The Brand Role is paired with a Connection statement that translates the role into a relational claim. The typology is treated in step 2.1.2.8 and stands as a tool any practitioner can apply when testing whether a proposed brand decision sits within the role the brand has claimed.


#### The positioning formula

WE HELP [target audience] WHO [pain-points] TO ACHIEVE / EXPERIENCE [key benefit] UNLIKE [competitive alternative] OUR SOLUTION [differentiator statement]. The formula is treated in step 2.1.2.9. It is also a coherence-testing tool any practitioner can apply at any later point in the engagement: if a proposed decision contradicts the positioning, the decision is challenged.


#### The seven-block story framework

Existing World → Obstacle → Call to Action → Meeting the Guide → Challenge → Transformation → New World. The framework is treated in step 2.1.2.11. It is also a reference tool for any later moment in the practice that requires the brand to tell its own story: founder interviews, press materials, the about page, the welcome address to new staff.


#### The deep brand interrogation

Eleven open questions, treated in step 2.1.2.12, that surface brand character at a level of specificity sufficient to produce naming candidates and to refine the strategy built earlier. The interrogation is also a useful diagnostic — if a brand cannot answer the questions at length, the strategy has work left to do.


#### The designer selection checklist

Processes, Language, Variation, Style Guide, Interview, The Brief. The checklist is treated in step 2.1.2.14. It applies to the visual identity designer specifically, but the principles generalise — the same six criteria, adapted, apply to any external partner the brand engages.


#### The Persona in Practice template

The nine-layer template used at step 2.1.2.8 Instrument 6: persona core, traits, tone sliders, moment and touchpoint map, do and don’t examples, word rules, hard moments, final checklist, and operational link, followed by the assembly of the AI Persona Brief. The template is uploaded to the client project once Instruments 1 to 5 are settled. It is also a review tool at any later point: a draft, a script, or an AI output that fails the Layer 8 checklist is returned to the layer where it went wrong.


#### Behavioural economics and non-verbal communication

Both libraries (Part III) are referenced at Stage 1 in two places. The Audience Persona uses behavioural economics to make sense of the Emotional Insights layer — how the audience actually decides, what biases shape their experience of the category. The Brand Personality uses non-verbal communication to anchor the Personality Expression dimensions — particularly Physical Appearance, Dress, Tone of Voice, Volume, and Attitudes — in observable, designable behaviour.


## 2.1.5 Patterns

This section holds the patterns the practice has accumulated at Stage 1. At version one, the section is seeded with universal patterns. It grows with every engagement, as new patterns are harvested and added.

Each pattern is presented in a uniform form: when to use it, what it produces, what it asks of the brand, and the failure mode.


#### Pattern S1-01 — The reduction discipline at Core Values

When to use: when the founder has produced a long list of values during Worksheet 4. Most founders generate eight to twelve.

What it produces: a reduced list of three to five values, each with an operational statement. The reduction is achieved by repeated forced choice: “if you could keep only five, which five.”

What it asks of the brand: the willingness to let go of values that sound right but that the brand would not actually defend under pressure.

Failure mode: a reduction that keeps the list short but loses the operational specificity. The shortened list must still produce statements that test in operation.


#### Pattern S1-02 — The 70/30 archetypal mix

When to use: at both the Audience Persona and the Brand Personality steps, whenever a single archetype is being resisted by the founder or the persona requires more texture than one archetype provides.

What it produces: a primary identity at roughly 70 percent (the Core Archetype) and a secondary influence at 30 percent or less (the Influencer Archetype). Characteristics are captured separately, not merged. The Core is what the audience or brand presents as; the Influencer is the texture that softens or sharpens the Core.

What it asks: the willingness to commit to one primary identity while acknowledging the secondary register. The brand or persona reads as one thing first.

Failure mode: a 50/50 split, which produces an identity that reads as inconsistent or undecided. If the founder cannot tip toward a primary, the interrogation continues.


#### Pattern S1-03 — The Role/Connection coherence test

When to use: at the close of step 2.1.2.8, when the Brand Role has been selected and the Connection statement has been drafted, before moving to Positioning.

What it produces: a coherence check across three prior interrogations. The Brand Role must respond to the audience’s Personality Desires established at step 2.1.2.8 Instrument 1. The Connection statement must use the language register the audience uses. The brand’s Archetypal Mix must produce a brand that can credibly occupy the chosen Role.

What it asks of the brand: that the relational position is not aspirational but operable. A brand whose Role is Guide must actually have something to guide toward; a brand whose Role is Defender must actually have something to defend against.

Failure mode: a Role chosen for its appeal rather than for its fit. The test is whether the Connection statement reads as something a customer would recognise being said to them.


#### Pattern S1-04 — The strategy-not-copy discipline at message blocks

When to use: in step 2.1.2.10 (Core Message Blocks), when the founder or the writer begins producing finished copy in the message-block statements.

What it produces: a clean distinction between message strategy (what the audience should take away) and message copy (the words that deliver the takeaway). The blocks hold strategy; the copy is downstream.

What it asks of the brand: that it resists the temptation to write the marketing material now. Message blocks that contain finished copy collapse the architecture; the copy ages quickly, and the strategy ages with it.

Failure mode: a Primary Core that reads as marketing slogans. If any block reads like something that could appear in an ad, it is rewritten as strategy.


#### Pattern S1-05 — The deep interrogation as diagnostic

When to use: when the strategy appears complete but feels surface-level, or when the brand is reaching the Name step and the keywords feel thin.

What it produces: the eleven deep interrogation questions, answered at length. The answers either confirm the strategy is grounded or surface gaps that require returning to earlier steps.

What it asks of the brand: time and honesty. The questions cannot be answered in a single sitting and cannot be answered with the language of marketing.

Failure mode: answers that are short and brand-flavoured. If the answers read as material from a website’s About page, the interrogation has been performed rather than conducted.


## 2.1.6 Briefs generated

Stage 1 generates the first external partner briefs of the engagement. The designer brief is mandatory for any engagement that involves a new visual identity and is generated by the Designer Selection step directly. The architect brief and the interior designer brief are extensions of the practice — they apply where the engagement includes a physical space and are not specified by the Branding Framework itself, but follow the same principles.

Each brief is written by VIOS. The partner may be one the practice has worked with before or one the client brings; the brief is the same. The principle is that the brand is held at the practice, and the brief is the instrument through which it reaches the partner.


#### The designer brief


#### The designer brief follows directly from the Designer Selection step (2.1.2.14). It contains the full brand book — Purpose, Vision, Mission, Values, Audience Persona (including the Archetypal Mix and the Brand Affinity Audit), Differentiator, Brand Personality (including Brand Role, Connection statement, Archetypal Mix with Core/Influencer Characteristics, Personality Expression, Brand Identity Notes, and Persona in Practice), Positioning Statement, Core Message Blocks, Story Framework, Brand Name, and Tagline if adopted. The designer reads the brand book as primary input.

In addition to the brand book, the designer brief contains a specific section on what the visual identity must achieve, what it must not do, the applications it will be used in (signage, menu, digital, print, packaging, staff materials), and any constraints inherited from the room (where the room is being designed in parallel). The Brand Identity Notes are reproduced as a dedicated section so the designer can locate them quickly.

The brief does not contain finished design references or mood direction beyond the Brand Identity Notes and the visual reference set. The designer is being given strategy and direction; the solution is theirs to reach.

Typical length: four to eight pages of brief, plus the brand book as appendix.


#### The architect brief

Where the engagement includes a building or major renovation, an architect brief is generated. The brief contains the brand book in summary form (Purpose, Vision, Mission, Audience Persona, Brand Role and Connection, Brand Personality Expression), plus a section on the spatial implications of the brand.

Spatial implications cover: orientation, sequence, acoustic character, the geometry of arrival and departure, sightlines, the relationship between guest-visible and back-of-house spaces, the operational adjacencies the brand requires (e.g., a brewery that is visible to guests, a kitchen that is open or closed), the sensory dominants the brand requires the room to deliver. The brief gives the architect the constraints, not the design.

Typical length: four to eight pages plus brand book appendix.


#### The interior designer brief

Where the engagement includes a finished room, an interior designer brief is generated. The brief contains the brand book in summary form, plus a section on the sensory implications of the brand — material principles, lighting principles, sound principles, scent principles, surface principles — at the level of brand implication, not at the level of specification.


#### The interior designer brief acknowledges its relationship to the architect brief (where the engagement has both partners working in parallel). The brand defines what the room must feel like; the architect defines what the room must do; the interior designer reconciles the two in the physical execution.

Typical length: four to seven pages plus brand book appendix.


#### Common structure across briefs

All three briefs follow the same structure: a one-paragraph summary of the brand, a section on what the brand requires, a section on what the brand refuses, a section on what the brand is indifferent to, a section on the specific decisions the partner is being asked to make, and a section on the review and revision process.

The indifference section is often missed and is important. A brief that names only requirements and refusals appears to constrain everything; the partner cannot tell where they have latitude. Naming what the brand does not care about — explicitly — gives the partner a clear field within which to work.

Each brief includes a stated revision protocol. Briefs are not final on issue. The partner’s first response may surface tensions the brief did not anticipate, and the brief may be revised in response. Revisions are versioned and dated. The version in force at any point is the version both parties are working from.


## 2.1.7 Harvest

The harvest at Stage 1 is the discipline of contributing what the engagement taught back to the practice. It is run at the close of the stage — not at the close of the engagement — because Stage 1 outputs are stable once produced and the lessons are clearest while they are fresh.

The harvest produces three outputs.


#### New patterns

Any pattern the engagement used that is not yet in section 2.1.5 is written up in the standard form: when to use, what it produces, what it asks of the brand, failure mode. New patterns are added to section 2.1.5 as part of the next version increment of the manuscript.

The threshold for recording a new pattern is one successful use; the threshold for promoting it from candidate to confirmed is two.


#### Framework revisions

Any change to the worksheets or the Reference Tool that the engagement suggested is noted. The framework is the methodology’s working instrument; it is revised only with stated reason and only after a pattern has emerged across more than one engagement. A revision is a deliberate act, logged in the version control discipline (Part V).


#### Instrument 6 (Persona in Practice) is provisional at version 1.3. It was entered on practitioner authority, ahead of engagement evidence. At every Stage 1 harvest, record which of the nine layers held, which were thin, and which failed in use. The instrument is confirmed once two engagements have used it; until then, its layers may be revised on the evidence of a single engagement.


#### Library entries

Any new application of behavioural economics or non-verbal communication that emerged in the stage is added to the relevant library in Part III. The Brand Personality work (step 2.1.2.8) is particularly likely to generate new applications, because each engagement adds a worked example of how a particular archetype combination expresses in a particular hospitality concept — and how a particular Brand Role plays out across the eight Personality Expression dimensions.

The harvest is short — an hour or two at stage close, not a separate project. Its purpose is to make sure the work the practice did once is available to the practice next time, without the practitioner having to remember to look for it.
