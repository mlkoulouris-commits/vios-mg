## 2.4 Stage 4 — Training Material & Programme


## 2.4.1 What this stage does

Stage 4 translates the designed experience into staff capability. The brand exists, the journey is mapped, the moments are designed — all of which is true on paper and useless on the floor until the people who run the operation can do it. Stage 4 produces the documents and the cadence by which they do it.

This stage is structurally different from the three that precede it. Stages 1, 2, and 3 produced material for the practice and its partners — written in the practice's voice, organised for strategic reference, optimised for designers and architects and operators who can read documents. Stage 4 produces material for staff. Material for staff is read under different conditions: in fragments, between shifts, in the half-hour pre-shift meeting, on the floor with an apron on, sometimes mid-service with a guest already at the table. It must be written for those conditions, not for the conditions in which the brand book was read.

The translation work at this stage is the most demanding of any in the practice. The brand book is in the concept register; the design pages are in the operational register; the staff materials must be in the floor register. The three-register pattern (Part III, section 3.3) is the engine of the stage. Translation is not paraphrase. A floor-register version of an operational instruction reads as a different document with a different rhythm and a different vocabulary. Staff who receive operational-register material as their training will execute it as theatre. Staff who receive floor-register material trained from operational-register source will execute it as themselves.

Stage 4 also produces the cadence — the schedule by which materials are introduced, practised, reinforced, and refreshed. A training programme is not a set of documents handed to a manager on day one. It is a sequence over weeks (in pre-opening) and a rhythm over months (after opening). The cadence is part of the programme; without it, the materials are reference works that staff consult occasionally, instead of an apparatus that produces capability.

The output of Stage 4 is the training programme: a stack of staff-facing documents (facilitator guides, workbooks, role cards, wall charts, readers, and the floor playbook), a stack of management-facing documents (the operating handbook, the coaching guides, the assessment instruments), and the cadence document that schedules them. The whole stack is delivered at the end of the engagement and runs the operation from there.


## 2.4.2 Method — building the training programme

The method has seven moves. The first is the intake from Stage 3. The second is the translation work that runs through everything else. The third decides which documents to produce. The fourth sets the cadence. The fifth writes the materials themselves. The sixth builds the coaching infrastructure that keeps the materials alive after opening. The seventh closes the programme.


### 2.4.2.1 Inputs from Stage 3

Stage 4 begins with the full Stage 3 deliverable. The design pages, the choreography document, and the recovery document are read together. The brand book and the journey map are also held available — the training materials must remain anchored to the brand strategy and the journey logic, not just to the design pages.

Four artefacts from Stage 3 are central:

The design pages. Every moment that staff will perform has a design page. The design page is the source from which the staff-facing material is translated. Stage 4 does not invent design; it translates Stage 3's design into a form staff can use.

The choreography document. Transitions, handoffs, and continuities are largely invisible in moment-by-moment training; they have to be made visible through the cadence and the coaching, or staff will perform individual moments cleanly and the joins between moments will collapse.

The recovery document. Recoveries are usually under-trained in hospitality and the practice corrects that here. Recovery scenarios receive their own training time and their own materials.

The hospitality concept. The menu, the beverage programme, the operational systems. Staff need to know the operation as deeply as they need to know the brand — and arguably more deeply. The practice does not produce the menu or the operational systems, but it builds them into the training materials in the floor register.

*Sufficient intake: all four artefacts read and held as working context. The intake produces a translation checklist — a list of every moment, recovery, and operational element that must be translated into floor-register material.*


### 2.4.2.2 Translate across registers

The translation discipline is the spine of Stage 4. It is treated in full depth in Part III, section 3.3. Here, the rules of translation as they apply specifically to training material are stated.

Material moves across three registers.


#### The concept register

The brand book and the engagement strategy documents are written in the concept register. The concept register is abstract, deliberate, and confident. It explains why decisions were made and how they connect to the brand. It is the right register for strategic conversation between the practice and the founder. It is the wrong register for floor execution.


#### The operational register

The design pages, the choreography document, and the recovery document are written in the operational register. The operational register is specific, instructional, and complete. It describes what is done and what the role of each element is. It is the right register for management — for the people who will train staff and supervise execution. It is still not the right register for floor execution.


#### The floor register

The training materials staff actually use — workbooks, role cards, wall charts, floor playbook — are written in the floor register. The floor register is short, declarative, and unmistakable. It does not explain; it instructs. It does not reason; it tells. It assumes the reader is in the middle of a shift and has approximately fifteen seconds to extract what they need.

Examples of the three registers carrying the same idea:

Concept register: "The brand refuses the hospitality default of effusive welcome, because effusion implies the guest needs to be reassured of their welcome — a posture inconsistent with the Quiet Refuser character."

Operational register: "Greeting at threshold: deliver a single locked line in a neutral-warm tone. Do not embellish. Do not ask if the guest has a reservation in the greeting — that exchange follows the greeting, not within it."

Floor register: "Welcome — sit anywhere. Wait one beat. Then ask about the reservation."

The three say the same thing. They do not say it in the same words. The translation discipline is that each register is written for its reader, not paraphrased from another register. A floor-register instruction is not a compressed operational instruction; it is a redrafted thing in its own voice and rhythm.

*Sufficient translation: every staff-facing piece of material has been written in the floor register, not adapted from the operational register. The voice and rhythm match the floor.*


### 2.4.2.3 Decide the document architecture

The document architecture is the decision about which documents are produced, who receives each, and how they relate to each other. The decision is made before any material is written, because writing the documents without knowing how they fit together produces materials that overlap or contradict each other.

The standard VIOS document architecture has two stacks: a staff-facing stack and a management-facing stack. The stacks are separate. Mixing them produces documents that try to do too many jobs at once and fail at all of them.


#### The staff-facing stack

Staff workbooks. One workbook per role (server, host, bartender, runner, kitchen). Each workbook contains the moments that role performs, in floor-register language, with the script, the non-verbal frame, and the success metric for each moment. Workbooks are short — typically twenty to forty pages, with substantial white space. They are written to be read in fifteen-minute increments.

Role cards. Pocket-sized laminated cards for each role, distilled from the workbook. The role card carries only the essentials: the locked scripts, the moments of decision, the recovery triggers. Role cards are read on the floor mid-shift.

Wall charts. Single-page references mounted in back-of-house areas. The wall chart typically carries the journey arc at a glance, with the locked scripts and the moments-of-truth marked. Wall charts are scanned in seconds, not read.

Readers. Optional. Some staff (often senior front-of-house, sommeliers, bar leads) benefit from a longer document that explains the why behind the floor-register instructions. The reader sits in the operational register — it is the bridge for staff who want to understand the design beneath the instruction. Readers are not standard; they are produced where the operation has staff who will use them.

The floor playbook. A single bound document, kept on the floor, that contains the locked scripts, the recovery triggers, and the moments-of-truth in floor register. The floor playbook is the staff's emergency reference — what they consult mid-service when memory fails.


#### The management-facing stack

Facilitator guides. One guide per training module. The facilitator guide is what the trainer (often a manager) uses to deliver the training. It contains the operational-register explanation, the activities, the discussion prompts, the assessment criteria. Facilitator guides are read in advance of training, not during it.

The operating handbook. The single management reference that contains the brand book summary, the journey map, the recovery design, and the operational standards. Managers consult it for the why behind any instruction. Staff do not see it.

Coaching guides. One per coaching topic. Coaching guides give managers the language to coach specific behaviours — the locked greeting, the silent walk, the recovery from a complaint. Coaching guides are operational-register; they sit on the manager's side.

Assessment instruments. Forms, checklists, and observation rubrics by which managers assess whether staff are performing the moments to standard. Used in pre-opening certification and in periodic floor audits.

Not every engagement requires every document in the architecture. The standard set is the starting point; documents may be omitted where the operation will not use them. Omissions are deliberate, recorded, and explained in the engagement file.

*Sufficient architecture: every document named, audience identified, register specified, length range estimated. The architecture is approved by the founder and the operations team before writing begins.*


### 2.4.2.4 Set the cadence

Cadence is the schedule by which materials are introduced, practised, reinforced, and refreshed. Cadence has two phases: pre-opening and post-opening.


#### Pre-opening cadence

Pre-opening cadence runs for four to six weeks in the standard programme, compressed to ten to fourteen days where the operation cannot afford the longer ramp. The cadence is sequenced so that brand precedes journey, journey precedes design, design precedes execution.

Week 1 — Brand and audience. Staff learn who the brand is, who the customer is, what role the brand plays for the customer. Material: a short reader (operational register) for senior staff, a workbook chapter (floor register) for all staff. Outcome: staff can describe the brand and the audience without referring to the materials.

Week 2 — The arc and the moments. Staff learn the journey map: the phases, the moments, the weights. The peak and the end are named explicitly. Material: the wall chart introduced, the workbook chapter on the arc. Outcome: staff can name the moments in their role's path.

Week 3 — The designs. Staff learn the moments-of-truth in detail: scripts, non-verbal frames, success metrics. Heavy practice in pairs. Material: workbook chapters on the moments, role cards distributed. Outcome: staff can perform the moments to standard in role-play, with the facilitator observing.

Week 4 — Recovery and choreography. Staff learn the recoveries and the transitions/handoffs. Heavy practice in groups. Material: the recovery section of the workbook, the choreography section. Outcome: staff can recover from common failure scenarios and pass guests between roles without dropping the journey.

Weeks 5-6 — Integration and certification (where the cadence has six weeks). Full-journey rehearsals with friends-and-family services. Assessment instruments applied. Staff who pass certification are cleared to work; staff who do not pass receive targeted additional coaching.


#### Post-opening cadence

Post-opening cadence is the rhythm by which the programme stays alive after the operation opens. It has three components:

Pre-shift meetings. Daily, fifteen to twenty minutes. The pre-shift meeting is a ritual that reinforces the journey and addresses the previous shift's lessons. Pre-shifts follow a standard agenda built into the facilitator guides: one moment from the journey reviewed, one recovery scenario walked through, anything specific to tonight (specials, large parties, anticipated issues).

Weekly coaching. Each manager spends focused coaching time with each direct report once a week. The coaching guides specify the topics in rotation: brand awareness, moment execution, recovery handling, choreography. Weekly coaching is fifteen to thirty minutes per staff member.

Monthly refreshers. Once a month, a single training module is run again — typically thirty to sixty minutes for the whole team. The module is chosen from a standing rotation, with rotation accelerated where assessment reveals gaps. Monthly refreshers prevent drift.

*Sufficient cadence: pre-opening sequence specified week by week with deliverables and outcomes; post-opening rhythm specified with frequency, duration, and content. The cadence is documented in a single cadence calendar that ships with the programme.*


### 2.4.2.5 Write the materials

Once the architecture and cadence are settled, the materials are written. The writing follows the architecture — each document in its specified register, for its specified audience, at its specified length.

Three writing disciplines apply across all materials:


#### Discipline 1 — One document, one job

Each document does one thing. A workbook is for staff to learn from in fifteen-minute increments. A role card is for staff to consult on the floor. A facilitator guide is for trainers to prepare from. When a single document tries to be a learning resource and a floor reference and a trainer's guide, all three jobs are done poorly. The architecture decision (2.4.2.3) named which document does which job; the writing must hold that line.


#### Discipline 2 — Show the design, do not summarise it

Floor-register materials show what is done; they do not summarise the design that produced it. A workbook chapter on the greeting moment does not begin with a paragraph explaining the Quiet Refuser character. It begins with the script and the geometry and the success metric. The why is in the reader (for those who want it) and in the facilitator guide (for those delivering the training); it is not in the workbook.

Staff who want the why can ask; the facilitator answers, drawing on the operational register. Staff who do not want the why have not been forced to read it before they can find the instruction.


#### Discipline 3 — Test by reading aloud

Floor-register material is tested by being read aloud. If a line is hard to say, it is rewritten. If a script does not flow into the action it precedes, the rhythm is wrong and the script is rewritten. Floor register is spoken language even when written down, because it will be spoken — by trainers in training, by staff in practice, and by the materials themselves when staff read them in the moment.

Materials that pass the read-aloud test are typically forty to sixty percent shorter than first drafts. The shortening is not loss; it is the removal of operational-register residue that staff would have to translate themselves.

*Sufficient writing: every document drafted in the correct register, read aloud where applicable, reviewed against the design pages and the brand book for accuracy. Drafts circulate to the founder and the operations team for review before the materials are finalised.*


### 2.4.2.6 Build the coaching infrastructure

Coaching infrastructure is the set of practices that keeps the training alive after the engagement closes. Without coaching infrastructure, the programme decays: the workbooks gather dust, the role cards are not refreshed, the pre-shift meetings drift into operational announcements, the brand erodes.

The infrastructure has four components:


#### The manager-as-coach standard

Managers are not floor supervisors. They are coaches whose primary job is to develop staff. The standard is established explicitly: managers are evaluated on the development of their staff, not only on the operation's nightly performance. This standard is treated in the operating handbook, but it is reinforced through the coaching guides and the assessment instruments.


#### The pre-shift ritual

The pre-shift meeting is a ritual, not a meeting. It has the same format every shift, opened by the manager, closed by the manager, focused on a single point from the journey or a single scenario from the recoveries. Staff who attend pre-shifts know what they are going to be asked to think about; the ritual is the apparatus of brand maintenance. A single facilitator guide is dedicated to the pre-shift ritual: how to run it, what to cover in rotation, how to respond when something has gone wrong in the previous shift.


#### The standing rotation

Topics rotate through the pre-shifts, the weekly coaching, and the monthly refreshers on a standing schedule. The schedule is in the cadence calendar. Standing rotation prevents the operation from training only on what went wrong last night and ignoring what is going right; the brand is maintained by attending to all of its moments, not just the broken ones.


#### The assessment loop

Assessment is built into the rhythm. Periodic floor audits, periodic table audits, periodic mystery-guest visits where commissioned. Findings feed back into the standing rotation: topics where assessment reveals gaps accelerate in rotation; topics that consistently pass slow. The assessment instruments produced in the engagement are designed to be run by managers on a periodic basis, not only by VIOS during pre-opening.

*Sufficient infrastructure: the four components specified and integrated into the cadence. The operation can run the infrastructure without the practice present.*


### 2.4.2.7 Close the programme

The programme is closed with a coherence test. The test asks six questions.

Has every moment in the journey design been translated into floor-register material that staff actually use? If gaps exist, they are filled.

Does each document in the architecture have one job and do only that job? If documents are trying to do two jobs, they are split.

Has the floor-register material been tested by reading aloud, and rewritten where it failed the test? If parts of the materials read as adapted operational register, they are rewritten.

Does the pre-opening cadence sequence brand → journey → design → execution, with appropriate time for practice and recovery? If the cadence is rushed, the timing is renegotiated with the operations team, even if it requires delaying the opening.

Does the post-opening cadence have the four coaching infrastructure components in place — manager-as-coach standard, pre-shift ritual, standing rotation, assessment loop? If any is missing, it is built before handoff.

Can the operation run the programme without the practice present? If the answer is no, the gap is identified and filled before handoff. The practice does not run the programme as a service; it builds the programme to be run by the operation itself.

The programme is closed when all six tests pass. Stage 4 — and the engagement — close together.


## 2.4.3 Template — the document architecture

The document architecture is the template at Stage 4. Unlike Stages 1, 2, and 3, where the template is a single document with internal structure, Stage 4's template is a set of documents with prescribed relationships. The template specifies what is in each, who reads each, in what register each is written, and what length range each occupies.


#### The staff-facing stack


#### Staff workbooks (one per role)

Audience: staff member in the role. Register: floor. Length: 20 to 40 pages. Contains: the moments performed by this role in journey order, with script, non-verbal frame, sensory frame (where relevant), success metric, and failure trigger for each. Plus: a brand summary at the front (one to two pages in floor register), a glossary at the back (key terms staff hear from management). Used: in pre-opening training, in onboarding new staff after opening.


#### Role cards

Audience: staff in the role. Register: floor. Length: one to two laminated pocket cards. Contains: locked scripts, recovery triggers, peak and end moments highlighted. Used: on the floor during service. Designed to be scanned in seconds while standing.


#### Wall charts

Audience: all staff. Register: floor. Length: one large-format sheet per chart. Contains: the journey arc with moments named, locked scripts highlighted, the brand summary in one or two sentences. Used: posted in back-of-house. Designed to be absorbed peripherally during the working day.


#### Readers (optional)

Audience: senior staff who want the design behind the instructions. Register: operational. Length: 30 to 50 pages. Contains: the journey design at operational depth, with cross-references to the brand book sections that govern it. Used: by senior staff for self-directed deeper understanding. Not standard.


#### The floor playbook

Audience: all front-of-house staff. Register: floor. Length: 30 to 60 pages, bound. Contains: locked scripts in journey order, recovery triggers and recovery scripts, moments-of-truth highlighted. Used: as the operation's standing reference. Kept at the host stand or in the manager's pocket. The most-consulted document after opening.


#### The management-facing stack


#### Facilitator guides

Audience: managers delivering training. Register: operational, with floor-register quotations of what trainees should say. Length: 15 to 30 pages per guide, with one guide per module in the pre-opening cadence and one for the pre-shift ritual. Contains: the design intent of the module, the activities, the discussion prompts, the assessment criteria, the time budget. Used: by managers in advance of delivering training.


#### The operating handbook

Audience: managers. Register: operational. Length: 80 to 150 pages. Contains: the brand book summary, the journey map and reasoning, the design pages in summary form, the recovery designs, the operational standards, the manager-as-coach standard, the assessment philosophy. The single management reference. Used: consulted whenever a manager needs the why behind any instruction.


#### Coaching guides

Audience: managers coaching individual staff. Register: operational. Length: 5 to 10 pages per guide, with one guide per coaching topic. Contains: the coaching topic, the language to use, the observations to make, the follow-up to schedule. Used: in weekly coaching sessions with direct reports.


#### Assessment instruments

Audience: managers conducting assessments. Register: operational, with observable behaviours stated in floor-register quotation marks. Length: 1 to 5 pages per instrument. Contains: the criteria, the observation method, the scoring rubric, the actions triggered by each score range. Used: in pre-opening certification, in periodic floor audits, in mystery-guest review.


#### The cadence calendar

A single document that schedules every training module, pre-shift theme, weekly coaching topic, and monthly refresher across a year. The calendar opens the engagement file and is updated as the operation runs. The cadence calendar belongs in the management-facing stack but is referenced from across both stacks; it is what makes the architecture run rather than sit on a shelf.


## 2.4.4 Toolkit

The toolkit at Stage 4 is the set of instruments used in producing the training programme. Each is named here briefly with cross-references.


#### The three-register translation pattern

Part III, section 3.3, treated in full depth. The translation engine of the stage. Used everywhere material moves from concept or operational register to floor register.


#### The non-verbal communication library

Part III, section 3.2. Used heavily in the workbook chapters on moments where the non-verbal frame is the dominant content. Staff materials that name proxemics, paralanguage, kinesics, and chronemics explicitly produce staff who can execute non-verbally; staff materials that leave these dimensions implicit produce staff who default to category norms.


#### The behavioural economics library

Part III, section 3.1. Used in the workbook chapters on moments involving customer decisions (ordering, choosing wine, deciding on dessert). Staff learn not just the scripts but the principles that make the scripts work — which is the basis for improvisation within the brand's character when the scripts do not exactly fit a situation.


#### The standard document set

The architecture specified in section 2.4.3 is itself the toolkit's central instrument. Variation from the standard is deliberate and documented; the architecture provides the default.


#### Coaching language patterns

A small set of standard coaching openers, closers, and feedback structures, drawn from coaching literature and adapted to hospitality. Used in coaching guides. Examples: the observation-impact-question opener ("I noticed X. The impact was Y. What were you thinking?"); the practice-rehearsal closer ("Let's run that moment again, once."). Pattern catalogue grows with the practice.


#### Pre-shift ritual templates

Two or three standing pre-shift formats, varied to prevent the ritual becoming rote. The standing-moment format: one moment from the journey reviewed in five minutes, including a role-play. The standing-scenario format: one recovery scenario walked through. The standing-customer format: one customer profile from the persona library reviewed, with how to recognise them on the floor and how to serve them well.


## 2.4.5 Patterns

This section holds the patterns the practice has accumulated at Stage 4. The patterns are about how training material is shaped, how cadence is timed, and how coaching is delivered.

Each pattern is presented in the standard form: when to use, what it produces, what it asks of the brand or the operation, failure mode.


#### Pattern S4-01 — The four-week pre-opening sequence

When to use: in engagements where the operation has four to six weeks before opening and can afford a standard pre-opening cadence.

What it produces: the brand → arc → design → execution → integration sequence specified in 2.4.2.4. Staff arrive at opening with the brand internalised, the journey understood, the moments practised, the recoveries rehearsed.

What it asks of the operation: the discipline of resisting accelerated training even when commercial pressure to open is high. Four weeks of training cost less than three months of recovering from a botched opening.

Failure mode: training compressed to fit a shorter pre-opening window, with the consequence that staff perform moments without internalising them, and the brand reads as theatre in the early weeks of operation.


#### Pattern S4-02 — The compressed pre-opening sequence

When to use: in engagements where the operation has ten to fourteen days before opening and a longer sequence is not possible.

What it produces: the same sequence as Pattern S4-01, compressed into shorter modules with heavier reliance on the workbook for self-study between sessions. Practice time is preserved; classroom time is reduced. Recoveries are introduced earlier in the sequence and reinforced more aggressively in the post-opening cadence.

What it asks of the operation: more intensive practice during the compressed sequence, more aggressive post-opening reinforcement to fill the gaps. Acknowledged as a second-best cadence; the operation accepts that the brand will be visible but rough for the first four to six weeks of service.

Failure mode: assumed equivalence to the four-week sequence. The compressed sequence produces a different shape of capability — strong on individual moments, weaker on choreography and recovery. The post-opening cadence must address the difference deliberately.


#### Pattern S4-03 — The first-thirty-days plan

When to use: in the post-opening cadence, for the first thirty days after the operation opens.

What it produces: an accelerated rhythm of pre-shift coaching, daily debriefs, and weekly check-ins between the practice and the operation. The first thirty days are when the design encounters reality; the rhythm is designed to surface and address the misfits quickly.

What it asks of the operation and the practice: the willingness to keep the practice engaged during the first month, even at additional cost. The investment is small relative to the cost of a brand that hardens in a wrong direction during the first weeks of operation.

Failure mode: the practice disengaging at opening, treating the engagement as complete when the doors open. Stage 4 properly closes thirty days after opening, not the day the operation opens.


#### Pattern S4-04 — The role card discipline

When to use: when staff workbooks are being produced and the temptation is to expand role cards into mini-workbooks.

What it produces: role cards that hold no more than the locked scripts, the recovery triggers, and the peak and end moments. Cards that go beyond this become unread.

What it asks of the practice: ruthless reduction. Anything that does not need to be checked mid-service is removed from the card. The workbook holds the depth; the card holds the essentials.

Failure mode: role cards that exceed two laminated panels. Staff stop carrying them; if they carry them, they stop consulting them.


#### Pattern S4-05 — The reading-aloud rewrite

When to use: when floor-register material is being drafted, especially scripts and pre-shift content.

What it produces: material that has been read aloud and rewritten where the reading exposed friction. Scripts that flow into action; instructions that fit the rhythm of a real conversation; pre-shift content that a manager can deliver in fifteen minutes without sounding like they are reading a document.

What it asks of the practice: time. Reading aloud takes longer than visual review, and the rewrites that result lengthen the writing phase by twenty to thirty percent. The investment is justified by what the materials produce in operation.

Failure mode: floor-register material that reads correctly on the page but breaks when staff try to speak it. The break is invisible to a reader who has not read the material aloud.


#### Pattern S4-06 — The pre-shift as ritual, not meeting

When to use: in the post-opening cadence, when the pre-shift meeting is being designed and the operation defaults to using it for operational announcements.

What it produces: a pre-shift that opens with a brand-relevant point (one moment from the journey, one scenario from the recoveries, one persona from the audience), runs the operational announcements after the brand point, and closes with a brief team-readiness check. The brand point comes first because what is first sets the frame for the shift.

What it asks of the operation: the discipline of holding the brand point at the opening of the pre-shift even on nights where operational announcements feel urgent. The discipline is what makes the pre-shift a ritual rather than a meeting.

Failure mode: pre-shifts that drift toward operational-announcement format, with the brand point omitted or relegated to closing. Within weeks the pre-shift loses its function as brand maintenance and becomes another operational meeting.


#### Pattern S4-07 — The manager-as-coach evaluation

When to use: in the operating handbook and the assessment infrastructure, when the manager evaluation system is being established.

What it produces: an evaluation that weighs the development of direct reports as at least equal to operational performance. Managers are not floor supervisors; they are coaches. The evaluation makes the standard visible and consequential.

What it asks of the operation: a real shift in how managers are recruited, evaluated, and promoted. Operations that say they value coaching but evaluate managers only on cover counts produce floor supervisors. Operations that evaluate managers on coaching produce coaches.

Failure mode: the standard stated but not enforced. Within months the operation reverts to evaluating managers on what is easy to measure (cover counts, complaint volumes) and the coaching infrastructure decays.


## 2.4.6 Briefs and handoffs

Stage 4 does not generate external partner briefs in the sense that Stages 1 and 3 do. The training programme is produced by the practice directly. What Stage 4 generates instead is a set of handoffs to the operation — the documents and the relationships by which the operation takes the programme over and runs it after the engagement closes.

Three handoffs are common.


#### Handoff 1 — To the management team

The management team receives the management-facing stack at the end of pre-opening training. The handoff is not a meeting; it is a structured session in which each document is introduced, its purpose explained, its use demonstrated, and its first-month usage scheduled with the manager who owns it.

The handoff includes a question-and-answer session. Managers ask the practice what is not clear; the practice answers in operational register. Questions that recur across multiple engagements become candidates for the operating handbook to address explicitly in the next version.

The handoff is documented: which manager received which document, on what date, with which scheduled first use. The documentation closes the engagement file.


#### Handoff 2 — To the operations team

Where the engagement has an operations partner separate from the management team — for example, a kitchen team operating semi-independently from front-of-house — a distinct handoff is run for them. The materials they receive overlap with the management-facing stack but emphasise the operational standards that affect them specifically (timing, plate composition standards, kitchen-floor communication patterns).

This handoff is often shorter and more practical: cooks and bartenders read documents less than managers do, and the handoff is focused on the small set of standing documents they will actually use.


#### Handoff 3 — To the founder

The founder receives the full stack — staff-facing and management-facing — with a separate session that focuses on the brand book, the journey map, and the operating handbook. The founder is not a manager and does not need the coaching guides or the assessment instruments, but the founder owns the brand and must be able to recognise drift when it occurs.

The founder's handoff also includes the practice's recommendation for the first-thirty-days plan: how often the practice should be consulted, what signals to bring to the practice in the first month, what to do if the operation begins to drift from the design. The plan is short and recorded; it is the closing document of the engagement.

None of these handoffs is a brief in the sense of the architect or designer briefs from earlier stages. They are transfers of capability. The documents are not given to be executed by external partners; they are given to be operated by the operation itself.


## 2.4.7 Harvest

The harvest at Stage 4 closes the engagement. Because Stage 4 is the last stage, its harvest is also the engagement-level harvest — the moment at which the whole engagement is reviewed for what it taught the practice.

The harvest produces five outputs.


#### New patterns at Stage 4

Any pattern the engagement used in training, cadence, or coaching that is not yet in section 2.4.5 is recorded. Stage 4 patterns travel readily across engagements because hospitality concepts vary more in their menus than in their training needs; a pre-shift ritual that worked in one operation usually works in another with light adaptation.

Document template refinements

Any change to the staff-facing or management-facing document templates the engagement suggested is noted. Templates at Stage 4 are particularly impactful because the documents are reused with light adaptation across engagements; a refinement to the workbook structure saves time on every subsequent engagement.


#### Library entries

Stage 4 generates fewer library entries than Stage 3 but adds important ones — particularly in the non-verbal communication library, where coaching applications surface dimensions of NV that the design pages had not isolated explicitly. Stage 4's harvest into the libraries focuses on coaching-ready treatments of principles: how to teach proxemics, how to coach paralanguage.


#### Engagement-level retrospective

At the close of the last stage of the engagement, the practice conducts a retrospective across all four stages. Three questions: what worked, what did not, what would be done differently in the next engagement. The retrospective produces a short document that is filed with the engagement archive and that informs the next engagement's intake (Part V).

Engagement-level retrospective is the moment at which the practice as a whole improves. Patterns at any stage may have been recorded in their stage-specific harvests; the retrospective asks the higher-level question of whether the practice itself — the four-stage architecture, the three-layer system, the version control of this manuscript — needs revision.


#### Worked example candidate

The retrospective also produces a recommendation on whether the engagement merits a Part IV case. The criterion (Part IV, section 4.1) is whether the engagement taught the practice something. If yes, the case is scheduled to be written; if no, the engagement contributes to patterns, libraries, and templates but not to the case studies. The recommendation is recorded; the decision to actually write the case is made later, when there is distance from the engagement and the lesson can be stated cleanly.

The harvest at Stage 4 is the longest of the practice — typically a full day or more, including the engagement-level retrospective. The investment is the price of compounding; without it, each engagement is a fresh start.
