## 3.1 Behavioural Economics for Hospitality

## 3.1.1 Why this library exists

Behavioural economics is the study of how humans actually decide, as distinct from how they would decide if they were the rational actors of classical economics. The field has produced a body of work — Kahneman, Tversky, Thaler, Cialdini, and many others — that names the systematic patterns in human decision-making and shows how environment shapes those patterns.

Hospitality is a decision-dense environment. A customer walking into a restaurant makes dozens of decisions in the space of two hours: whether to sit at this table or that one, what to order, how much to drink, whether to add a side, whether to stay for dessert, how much to tip, whether to come back. Each decision is shaped by the surrounding environment — the menu's layout, the staff's language, the room's geometry, the pace of the service. A brand that understands which decisions the customer is making and how those decisions are shaped can design moments that work with the customer's cognition rather than against it.

This library is the practice's working subset of behavioural economics. It draws primarily on Daniel Kahneman's Thinking, Fast and Slow — the most rigorous and complete treatment of cognitive biases in the literature — supplemented by Malcolm Gladwell's Blink for its treatment of rapid judgment, by Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein's work on choice architecture and nudges, and by Robert Cialdini's work on influence. Where a principle has a clear primary source it is cited; the practice does not pretend these are its own discoveries.

The library is selective. The general literature is larger; many principles in it do not have a reliable, useful application in hospitality and have been excluded. The principles included here are each ones the practice has used repeatedly with consistent enough effect that they can be taught and applied with confidence.

The library is also written for application. Each principle is described not in the language of academic psychology but in the language of moments — what the principle does to a customer in a dining room, a bar, a hotel lobby. The mechanisms are explained because understanding why a principle works is what makes its application robust.

This library belongs alongside the four stages. It is referenced from every stage. The brand book uses it to set voice and presence rules. The journey map uses it to assign weight — peak-end logic and the deeper framework of the two selves are the engines of the weight assignment move. The journey design uses it at every moment involving customer decisions. The training programme uses it to coach — staff who understand why the design works can sustain it without supervision.

## 3.1.2 How to use it

This library has three levels.

The first is the framework — System 1 and System 2 (section 3.1.3). This is the cognitive architecture that underlies everything else. The framework is read once for orientation; it is not consulted moment by moment, but every principle in the library makes sense in its terms.

The second is the two-selves framework (section 3.1.4) — the experiencing self and the remembering self. This is the second foundational framework. It governs how customers form memory of the experience and decide whether to return, and it is the framework underneath peak-end logic. Like System 1 and 2, it is read once for orientation and held as background.

The third is the principles (section 3.1.5). Each principle is treated in the same five-field shape — Definition, Mechanism, Hospitality application, Worked illustration, Failure mode — so a practitioner reaching the library mid-design can find what they need in a known location.

Three modes of consultation are common.

Consultation by moment. The practitioner is designing a moment and asks: what decisions is the customer making here, and which principles are at play? The relevant principles are pulled, applied, and cross-referenced from the design page.

Consultation by problem. The practitioner is troubleshooting — a moment that is underperforming, an offer that is being declined, a recommendation that is not being taken. The principles are scanned for one whose mechanism explains the underperformance.

Consultation by audience. The principles' applications shift slightly by archetype. A Sage audience is more resistant to social proof than an Everyman audience; a Caregiver audience is more responsive to loss aversion framed around protecting someone else than to loss aversion framed around themselves.

The library is not a checklist. Applying every principle to every moment produces moments that read as manipulation. The discipline is restraint: at any moment, two or three principles are usually dominant, and the design focuses on those.

One general rule worth stating before the framework. Behavioural economics works best when its presence is invisible to the customer. A design that uses anchoring well is one the customer experiences as a clear recommendation, not as a sales technique. The principles are tools for serving the customer better, not for extracting more from them. Hospitality brands that use behavioural economics to extract erode trust quickly; brands that use it to serve produce loyalty.

## 3.1.3 The foundation — System 1 and System 2

Kahneman organises his entire treatment of cognition around two systems of thinking. The systems are not literal parts of the brain; they are useful fictions that name two modes of mental operation. The whole library of biases sits inside this framework.

#### System 1

System 1 operates automatically and quickly, with little or no effort and no sense of voluntary control. It generates impressions and intuitions effortlessly. It detects hostility in a voice, completes the phrase "bread and...", makes a disgust face when shown something disgusting, drives a familiar car on an empty road, finds a strong move in chess if the player is a master. System 1's operations include innate skills (perceiving objects, orienting attention, fearing spiders) and learned skills that have become automatic through practice (reading, recognising stereotypes, executing well-learned procedures).

System 1 is fast, parallel, associative, and effortless. It produces a continuous stream of impressions, feelings, and intentions. Most of what we think and do originates in System 1. When we encounter a familiar situation, System 1 generates an answer immediately and we usually act on it.

System 1 has predictable biases — systematic errors it makes in specifiable circumstances. It substitutes easier questions for hard ones. It over-weights vivid information. It is influenced by stimuli the person did not consciously notice. It cannot be turned off, even when its outputs are known to be wrong. The famous Müller-Lyer illusion — two lines of identical length that appear different because of fins at their ends — continues to look different even after a ruler proves the lines equal. The seeing happens in System 1; the knowing happens in System 2; the seeing does not change.

#### System 2

System 2 allocates attention to the effortful mental activities that demand it. It performs complex computations, holds multiple items in working memory, monitors behaviour, suppresses impulses. It is the conscious, reasoning self that has beliefs, makes choices, and decides what to do. When we think of ourselves, we identify with System 2.

System 2 has one critical limitation: it requires effort, and effort is a limited resource. When System 2 is depleted — by other tasks, by stress, by alcohol, by decision fatigue — its capacity to monitor and override System 1 falls. The decisions made in this state are System 1 decisions, dressed in the appearance of deliberation.

System 2 is also lazy by default. It accepts System 1's suggestions unless they are obviously wrong or unless the situation explicitly invites careful thought. The phrase "jumping to conclusions" describes what happens when System 1 produces an answer and System 2 endorses it without examination — which is most of the time.

How the systems interact

Both systems are active whenever we are awake. System 1 runs continuously and generates suggestions for System 2 — impressions, intuitions, intentions, feelings. When all goes smoothly, which is most of the time, System 2 adopts the suggestions with little or no modification. The customer believes their impressions and acts on their desires.

System 2 is mobilised in specific situations. When a question arises for which System 1 does not offer an answer. When the person is surprised. When an error is about to be made. In these moments, System 2 takes over, slows the process down, and produces a more careful response. After the moment of effort, System 2 returns to its default state and System 1 resumes governance.

The implication for hospitality is that customers spend most of their time in System 1 — receiving the brand through impressions, reactions, feelings — and only briefly enter System 2 when the moment demands deliberation. Most of the design's work is done on System 1. The room's first impression, the staff member's tone, the menu's typography, the pace of the service — these are all processed by System 1, mostly outside the customer's awareness. The deliberate moments — choosing the wine, deciding on dessert, settling the bill — bring System 2 in briefly. The design must work for both modes.

Why this matters for the principles

Most of the principles in this library describe ways System 1 produces predictable patterns. Anchoring works because System 1 is influenced by available numbers even when System 2 knows the numbers are irrelevant. Framing works because System 1 receives the same information differently depending on how it is presented. Cognitive ease works because System 1 reads familiarity as truth. Defaults work because System 1 accepts what does not require active choice.

The principles are not failures of human reasoning. They are how the mind actually works. Designing with them is designing for human cognition as it is; designing against them is designing for a rational actor who does not exist. The discipline of the practice is to use the principles to serve the customer's experience, not to exploit a vulnerability.

## 3.1.4 The two selves — experiencing and remembering

Kahneman's second foundational distinction — independent of System 1 and System 2 — is between the experiencing self and the remembering self. The two selves do not always agree, and when they disagree, the remembering self wins.

#### The experiencing self

The experiencing self is the one that lives the moment. It answers the question "does it hurt now?" or "is this enjoyable now?" The experiencing self has direct access to the moment and reports on it in real time.

Most of a customer's time in a restaurant is spent in the experiencing self. The food is good now. The wine is right now. The conversation is alive now. The seat is comfortable, or it is not. The room is warm, or it is too cool. Each moment is registered as it occurs, by the experiencing self, who has no memory of the previous moment or expectation of the next.

#### The remembering self

The remembering self is the one that, after the experience, answers the question "how was it, on the whole?" The remembering self does not have access to every moment of the experience. It has a memory — a constructed, edited summary, generated by System 1 from the available data, weighted in ways the experiencing self would not endorse.

Two rules govern the remembering self's construction of memory.

The peak-end rule. The remembering self's evaluation of an experience is well predicted by the average of the most intense moment (the peak — positive or negative) and the final moment (the end). Other moments contribute little.

Duration neglect. The duration of the experience has almost no effect on the remembering self's evaluation. An hour of mild discomfort followed by relief is remembered as better than thirty minutes of the same mild discomfort with no relief at the end, even though the longer experience contains more total discomfort.

Together, peak-end and duration neglect mean the remembering self's report bears only a partial relationship to what the experiencing self actually went through. Two experiences of identical duration with identical average pleasure can be remembered very differently if their peaks and ends differ. Two experiences of different duration with identical peaks and ends can be remembered identically, even though one was much longer.

Which self decides whether to return

This is the question that makes the two-selves framework central to hospitality. The decision about whether to come back is made by the remembering self. The remembering self is what calls a friend the next day to recommend or warn off the restaurant. The remembering self is what writes the review. The remembering self is what holds the memory that produces, or fails to produce, repeat business.

A restaurant that delivers a wonderful experience to the experiencing self moment by moment, but lets the last fifteen minutes go thin, has produced a memory the remembering self will not recommend. The peak was good; the end was not; the average is mid; the verdict is mid. The customer who lived an excellent ninety minutes returns home with a mediocre memory.

Conversely, a restaurant whose middle is uneven but whose peak is strong and whose farewell is warm will be remembered well — and recommended — even if a moment-by-moment review of the experience would show as many low points as high points.

The implication for design is that hospitality is largely a business of producing good memory in the remembering self. The experiencing self matters — bad moments are bad for the customer who lives them — but the business question, the question of whether the customer comes back, is the remembering self's question, and the remembering self is governed by the peak and the end.

A cautionary note on the two selves

The two selves can be in conflict. A choice that serves the remembering self can shortchange the experiencing self. Kahneman illustrates this with a famous experiment: subjects who held their hand in cold water for 60 seconds remembered it as worse than holding their hand in cold water for 90 seconds, where the last 30 seconds were slightly less cold. Eighty percent of subjects, given the choice to repeat one of the two, chose to repeat the longer (objectively worse) experience. The remembering self misled them.

Hospitality design is not value-neutral on this question. A brand that engineers peak and end memory at the expense of the experiencing self's actual moments has chosen the remembering self's preference. This is sometimes the right choice (a marketing event that the customer will remember warmly is often more valuable than a marathon of uniform pleasantness) and sometimes the wrong one (a brand whose only strong moments are at peak and end and whose middle is hollow is a brand built on illusion). The design holds both selves in view; the brand's character governs the balance.

*Phrase to remember: the experiencing self lives the meal; the remembering self decides whether to return.*

## 3.1.5 The principles

Each principle is presented in the same five-field shape: Definition, Mechanism, Hospitality application, Worked illustration, Failure mode. Where a principle has a clear primary source it is cited.

### 1. Cognitive ease

*Source: Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow, Chapter 5*

#### Definition

Cognitive ease is the subjective sense that something is easy to process — to read, recognise, remember, or evaluate. When something is cognitively easy, the mind treats it as more familiar, more true, more trustworthy, more pleasant. When something is cognitively difficult, the mind treats it as suspect, unfamiliar, less true.

#### Mechanism

System 1 uses processing fluency as a proxy for truth and familiarity. The associative machinery treats ease of retrieval as evidence that the content has been encountered before, which is taken as evidence that it is real. Repeated exposure increases ease. Clear typography, simple sentences, rhyming pairs, familiar names — all increase ease. Hard-to-pronounce names, complex sentences, unfamiliar fonts decrease ease. The effects operate beneath awareness; people who are influenced by cognitive ease cannot tell that they are.

#### Hospitality application

Cognitive ease is at work everywhere the brand presents language or visual form: the menu's typography, the staff's vocabulary, the brand name itself, the visual identity, the room's signals of familiarity. A brand whose name is hard to pronounce starts with a deficit. A menu printed in a hard-to-read font is rated less trustworthy than the same menu printed in a clear font. A staff member who uses familiar phrasing is heard as warmer than a staff member who uses unusual constructions.

#### Worked illustration

Two restaurants in the same neighbourhood serve broadly similar food at similar prices. One has a name that is easy to say, a menu in a clean readable font, dishes named in plain language. The other has a name that requires explanation, a menu in a stylised difficult typeface, dishes named in language that demands translation. Customers thin-slice both before ordering. The first restaurant is judged warmer, more trustworthy, and more competent — not because of the food but because the surface is cognitively easier. The food has not yet arrived; the verdict is already partly formed.

#### Failure mode

Cognitive ease fails when the brand interprets ease as the goal rather than as a tool. A brand that flattens all friction in pursuit of ease becomes generic — it sounds like every other easy brand. The discipline is to know where ease serves the brand (a Quiet Refuser brand is heard more accurately when its language is plain than when it is studied) and where deliberate friction serves the brand (a brand whose character is the Sage may want its menu to require attention, because the audience seeks the depth signalled by the friction). The principle is descriptive; the brand decides where to align with it.

### 2. WYSIATI and substitution

*Source: Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow, Chapters 7 and 9*

#### Definition

WYSIATI — "What You See Is All There Is" — is the principle that the mind constructs the best possible story from the information available, and acts as if the information available were the whole story. Substitution is the related principle that when System 1 is asked a hard question, it silently swaps it for an easier one and answers the easier question.

#### Mechanism

System 1 builds coherent narratives from whatever data is in front of it. It does not assess whether the data is sufficient; it does not ask what might be missing. Coherence is the criterion, and a coherent story can be built from very little. The customer who sees a busy restaurant from the street has all they need to judge the restaurant — they will not ask whether the busyness is unusual or staged. The customer who reads a menu in a particular tone has all they need to judge the brand — they will not ask whether the tone is sustained across all menus, all nights, all staff.\\\\Substitution is what happens when System 1 cannot easily build a story for the question asked. The hard question is replaced by an easier one. "How happy are you with your life?" becomes "What is your mood right now?" "How was the meal?" becomes "How did the last few minutes feel?" The customer answers the easier question and believes they have answered the original.

#### Hospitality application

WYSIATI explains why first impressions are disproportionately powerful in hospitality. The customer constructs their model of the restaurant from the threshold moment, the host's tone, the room's first feel — and that model anchors everything that follows. Information that contradicts the model is harder to integrate than information that confirms it.\\\\Substitution explains why customer feedback is unreliable. "How was your evening?" is hard to answer accurately. The customer substitutes "How did the bill feel?" or "Was the last interaction warm?" and answers that. The feedback is then treated by the operation as evidence about the evening, when it is actually evidence about the last few minutes.

#### Worked illustration

A customer is asked to rate their meal on a scale of 1 to 10 immediately after the bill. They have just had a thirty-minute wait between courses that frustrated them, but the dessert was excellent and the farewell was warm. They rate the meal 9. The operation reads this as evidence the meal was outstanding. Two weeks later the customer has not returned and recommends the place tepidly to friends. The 9 was the answer to "how did the last few minutes feel" — which was high — not to "how was the meal" — which was mixed. Substitution produced a misleading score; WYSIATI made the operation treat the score as the whole picture.

#### Failure mode

WYSIATI fails when the operation treats limited information as complete information. The discipline is to know what is missing — what the customer did not see, what the staff did not say, what the room did not communicate — and to question whether the model the customer has formed is the model the brand intended. Substitution fails when feedback questions invite substitution without acknowledging it. Asking "how was the meal" produces substituted answers; asking specifically about each phase forces a more honest answer at the cost of more friction in the asking.

### 3. Anchoring

*Source: Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow, Chapter 11*

#### Definition

Anchoring is the cognitive pattern by which the first reference point a person encounters disproportionately shapes their subsequent judgements. Once an anchor is set, all later values are evaluated relative to it. The effect persists even when the anchor is known to be arbitrary or irrelevant.

#### Mechanism

Anchoring operates through two mechanisms. The first is a System 2 process of insufficient adjustment: faced with a question and an anchor, the person starts from the anchor and adjusts in the direction they believe to be correct, but stops too early — at the near edge of the range they consider possible. The second is a System 1 process of priming: the anchor activates compatible associations in memory, biasing subsequent judgements in the anchor's direction. Both mechanisms produce the same outward effect. The anchoring index — the proportion of the gap between high and low anchors that shows up in the resulting estimates — is typically around 50 percent. Real-estate professionals are influenced by listing prices to roughly the same degree as untrained students, while denying it.

#### Hospitality application

Anchoring operates most visibly in menu pricing and in the order in which options are presented. The first item the customer sees sets the anchor for what the rest of the menu costs. A wine list that opens with the most expensive bottle reframes every later bottle as reasonable. A tasting menu that opens with a one-hundred-twenty-euro option reframes the eighty-euro option as the sensible middle. Anchoring also operates in service: the first recommendation a server makes anchors the customer's expectation for what the meal will look like. It also operates in negotiation moments — the first price quoted in any negotiation (a private event, a large party booking) substantially shapes the eventual settlement.

#### Worked illustration

A wine list sorted from inexpensive to expensive anchors the customer at the cheapest bottle. The customer reads from the top, internalises the bottom of the price range, and judges everything else as more expensive than it needs to be. The same list sorted from expensive to inexpensive anchors the customer at the top, and the customer judges everything else as more reasonable than expected. The same wines, in different orders, produce different outcomes — typically a 30 to 50 percent shift in average bottle price chosen. A brand that wants its mid-range wines to be chosen often will anchor with a top-of-list price that makes the mid-range read as the considered choice.

#### Failure mode

Anchoring fails when the anchor is unbelievable. An anchor so high it reads as a stunt does not anchor — it is dismissed, and the customer's reference point reverts to their own prior expectation. The anchor must be plausible. A second failure mode: anchoring used so deliberately that the customer notices the technique. Customers who notice anchoring read the brand as commercial and the anchor itself as suspect; the effect inverts. The resistance strategy for anchoring is to instruct System 2 to actively search for arguments against the anchor — a defence the practice rarely needs to deploy but should be aware of when working with sophisticated counterparties.

### 4. Availability heuristic

*Source: Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow, Chapter 12*

#### Definition

The availability heuristic is the cognitive shortcut by which frequency or likelihood is judged based on how easily examples come to mind. Things that are easy to remember are estimated to be more frequent than things that are hard to remember.

#### Mechanism

System 1 substitutes the question "how often does this happen" with the easier question "how easily can I think of an example." Examples that are vivid, recent, emotional, or repeated come to mind easily, and the frequency they represent is over-estimated. Examples that are bland, distant, or unrepeated come to mind with difficulty, and the frequency they represent is under-estimated. People over-estimate the frequency of dramatic events (plane crashes, shark attacks) and under-estimate the frequency of mundane ones (car accidents, household falls).

#### Hospitality application

Availability operates in customer perception of the brand's quality, reliability, and reputation. A single bad night, vividly remembered, weighs more in the customer's overall sense of the brand than fifty good nights that have blurred together. A single instance of excellent service that a customer can recount in detail anchors their general estimate of the brand's service quality, even if the customer's other visits were average. Availability also operates within service moments: the wine the customer remembers the sommelier mentioning is the wine they consider most strongly, regardless of what else was on the list.

#### Worked illustration

A regular customer who has dined at the restaurant fifteen times had one bad evening — a wrong order, recovered well but vivid in their memory. Asked six months later how the restaurant is, they describe the bad evening in detail before mentioning the other fourteen. The availability heuristic has weighted the one vivid memory above the fourteen smooth ones. The recommendation they give to a friend is tinged with the one episode. The operation, knowing this, has two options: address the bad evening directly with the customer at the time (which converts the vivid memory into a memory of strong recovery, which is then also available), or accept that vivid bad memories will dominate over numerous bland good ones and design the bland good ones to be more memorable.

#### Failure mode

Availability fails when the operation assumes their own access to the data matches the customer's. The operation knows the fifteen good evenings; the customer remembers the one bad one. The customer's verdict is governed by availability, not by the operation's records. The operation that argues its case against the customer's availability loses. The operation that designs its recoveries and its peak moments to be vivid wins. The discipline is to know which memories the customer is forming and to invest in making the good ones as available as the bad ones.

### 5. Affect heuristic

*Source: Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow, Chapter 13; Slovic and colleagues*

#### Definition

The affect heuristic is the principle that emotional reactions to a thing shape judgements about that thing far more than people realise. The emotional response comes first, almost instantaneously; the cognitive evaluation follows and is largely a justification of the affect.

#### Mechanism

System 1 generates emotional responses to stimuli within milliseconds. The response is then used as input to all subsequent judgements about the stimulus. A customer who feels good about the room judges the food more favourably; a customer who feels uneasy about the room finds reasons the food was disappointing. The emotional valence colours the cognitive assessment. People believe they are evaluating the substance and then forming an emotional response, but the order is usually the reverse — the response is the substance, and the cognitive evaluation is the post-hoc rationalisation.

#### Hospitality application

Affect operates throughout the journey. The customer's first emotional read of the room — within seconds of entering — colours every subsequent moment of the evening. The customer who walks in and feels warmth in the room will find the food warmer, the service more attentive, the bill more reasonable. The customer who walks in and feels unease will find the same food cooler, the service distracted, the bill higher than expected. The room's first emotional read is therefore one of the highest-leverage moments in the design — small investments here multiply across the entire experience.

#### Worked illustration

Two customers visit the same restaurant on the same evening, served by the same staff, eating from the same kitchen. One enters during a moment when the room is alive — three other parties laughing, a glass clinking at the bar, the staff settled and attentive. The other enters during a brief lull — the room quiet, one party finishing their bill, the staff regrouping. Both have functionally identical service. The first customer remembers the evening as charming, generous, and considered; the second remembers it as flat and slightly absent. The food was the same. The affect at the threshold colored everything after.

#### Failure mode

Affect fails when the operation under-invests in the room's emotional first signal. Operations that focus on food quality and service procedure while leaving the room's emotional read to chance produce experiences whose ceiling is capped by whatever the customer happens to feel on entering. A second failure mode: affect engineered too aggressively — a host who performs warmth, a soundtrack that pushes liveliness — produces affect that registers as manufactured, which produces the opposite affect. The discipline is genuine warmth, not performed warmth; this is largely a Stage 1 and Stage 4 question, not a Stage 3 manipulation.

### 6. Defaults

*Source: Thaler and Sunstein, Nudge; supported by Kahneman's System 1/System 2 framework*

#### Definition

A default is the option that takes effect when the person does not actively choose. Defaults exert disproportionate influence because most people accept them — partly from inertia, partly from the implicit signal that the default is what the environment expects or recommends.

#### Mechanism

Active choice requires System 2 effort. A default removes the cost of choosing while still allowing the person to opt out. Most people do not opt out. The default also carries a subtle endorsement: the environment has named this option, which the person reads (often unconsciously) as the option the environment recommends. Defaults are perhaps the most powerful tool of choice architecture because they exploit System 1's preference for the path of no resistance.

#### Hospitality application

Defaults operate in many hospitality moments. The default starter, presented as the dish the kitchen leads with. The default pour, served when the customer says "a glass of wine" without specifying. The default bread, brought to the table without being asked. The default pace of service, determined by the kitchen without the customer being asked to choose. Each default shapes the customer's experience without requiring a decision; each is also a point at which the brand expresses itself.

#### Worked illustration

A brand whose differentiator is at the bar may set the default drink offer to a house cocktail rather than asking "what can I get you." The customer who would have ordered a generic spritz is offered the house version instead, with the option to redirect. The default is the brand's recommendation in the form of action; the customer who wants something else still has it. Over time, the default builds the bar programme's signature drinks into the operation's identity.

#### Failure mode

Defaults fail when they remove the customer's experience of choosing. A default service pace that is too aggressive feels rushed; a default amount of food that is too generous feels like the kitchen is performing. The customer's sense of being attended to depends partly on having choices they could have made differently. Defaults that pre-empt every choice produce a customer who feels handled rather than hosted.

### 7. Peak-end and duration neglect

*Source: Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow, Chapter 35 (Two Selves)*

#### Definition

The peak-end rule states that a person's memory of an experience is well predicted by the average of the peak (most intense moment, positive or negative) and the end (final moment). Duration neglect states that the duration of the experience has almost no effect on its remembered evaluation.

#### Mechanism

The remembering self (section 3.1.4) constructs the memory of an experience by sampling salient moments rather than integrating across the full experience. The peak and the end are the most reliably salient moments. Duration is not stored as a feature of the memory. Together, peak-end and duration neglect mean the remembered quality of an experience can be substantially different from the experienced quality, and the remembered quality is what governs the customer's decision to return.

#### Hospitality application

Peak-end logic is the engine of the weight assignment move in Stage 2. It also shapes Stage 3 directly: the peak moment and the end moment receive disproportionate design attention. The peak is usually identified in collaboration with the chef and the founder — what is the moment that the brand is most fully expressed, that the customer is most invited to be present for. The end is the farewell — and it is often under-designed because operational pressure to turn the table competes with the brand's interest in a strong end.\\\\Duration neglect has a counter-intuitive implication: a shorter strong meal will often be remembered better than a longer mixed one. Brands that have struggled with pacing sometimes find that the cure is shorter service, not longer.

#### Worked illustration

A restaurant with a strong food programme, strong service, a strong room — and a thin departure — produces guests who recall the evening as good rather than excellent. The peak (often the food's strongest course) is remembered; the end (the unceremonial bill, the absent farewell, the coat fetched without warmth) is also remembered. Investing fifteen minutes per shift in a deliberate farewell — a small token at the door, a moment of attention from a senior staff member, a remembered detail about the visit — moves the recalled quality of the experience up a tier with no change to the food or the service. The remembering self has been served.

#### Failure mode

Peak-end fails when applied mechanically. Not every brand should engineer a single dramatic peak; some brands' character is to deliver consistent quality without a single signature moment. A Quiet Refuser brand may deliberately not stage a peak, because peak-staging would be theatre the character refuses. The principle is descriptive of memory, not prescriptive of design. The design draws on peak-end where it serves the brand; it does not contort to peak-end where it would distort the brand. A second failure mode is exploiting duration neglect cynically — engineering a strong peak and end while the middle is hollow. Customers' experiencing selves notice the hollow middle even when their remembering selves forget it, and the inconsistency erodes the brand's authenticity over repeated visits.

### 8. Choice architecture

*Source: Thaler and Sunstein, Nudge*

#### Definition

Choice architecture is the design of the environment in which choices are presented. The number of options, their order, their grouping, their visual hierarchy, and the language used to describe them all shape which options are chosen.

#### Mechanism

Faced with options, people use the architecture of the presentation as input to their decision. Options grouped together are judged as similar. Options at the top of a list are weighted more heavily. Options described with more detail seem more considered. Options visually distinguished — boxed, highlighted, marked as "recommended" — are chosen more often, even when the underlying option is identical to the surrounding ones. System 1 reads the architecture as information about the options.

#### Hospitality application

Choice architecture is everywhere in hospitality. The menu is a structured presentation. The wine list. The cocktail list. The dessert offer. The upgrade offer. The room's geometry, which presents an architectural choice (sit at the bar, sit at a table, sit in the courtyard) before the customer has ordered. The discipline at every choice point is to ask: how is this choice presented, and is the presentation serving the customer's decision or hiding from it.

#### Worked illustration

A menu with thirty-two dishes as a single list overwhelms. The customer reads the first eight, scans the next ten, gives up on the rest, and orders from the first eight. The same thirty-two dishes grouped into three sections of roughly ten each — by preparation method, by main ingredient, by intensity — is navigable. The customer reads each section, makes choices within sections, and uses more of the menu. The kitchen's investment in the lesser-known dishes is rewarded. The choice architecture made the menu's full range visible to the customer.

#### Failure mode

Choice architecture fails when it constrains too aggressively. Removing the customer's sense of having chosen produces a customer who feels they were directed rather than offered. The architecture should make the right choices easy to find; it should not make wrong choices invisible. A menu in which only three options are presented with full description while twenty-nine are listed as bare names communicates that the three are mandatory; the customer feels the constraint.

### 9. Framing

*Source: Kahneman and Tversky; treated in Thinking, Fast and Slow, Chapter 34*

#### Definition

Framing is the principle that the same option, described in different terms, is perceived differently. The framing of an option — what is emphasised, what is omitted, the comparative reference invoked — shapes whether the option is accepted or rejected.

#### Mechanism

Different framings of the same factual content engage different cognitive responses. A dish described as "the chef's favourite" carries different weight from the same dish described as "the dish most people order." A wine described as "surprisingly affordable for the quality" carries different weight from the same wine described as "a budget option." Framing operates on the customer's sense of the option's value, the staff's character, and the brand's confidence. The most dramatic framing effects come from gain/loss reversal — the same option framed as a gain or as a loss produces substantially different choices, because of loss aversion (see principle 12).

#### Hospitality application

Framing operates in every staff script and every piece of menu copy. Hospitality is dense with framing decisions: how to describe a special, how to introduce a wine pairing, how to recover from a complaint, how to suggest dessert when the customer is hesitating. Each is a framing choice; the cumulative framing across an evening shapes the customer's sense of the brand more than any single piece of content.

#### Worked illustration

A staff member presenting tonight's special can frame it as a deliberate offer ("tonight the kitchen has built a dish around the asparagus that came in this morning — it's worth trying") or as a recovery default ("if you don't like the menu, there's also a special"). Both refer to the same dish. The first positions the dish as the brand's confidence; the second positions it as an apology for the menu. The dish does not change; what changes is the customer's reception.

#### Failure mode

Framing fails when the framing exceeds what the dish or the moment can deliver. A dish framed as the chef's favourite that arrives indifferently prepared damages the brand more than a neutrally framed dish that arrives well. Over-framing — language that promises more than the substance provides — produces backlash. A second failure mode: framing that is inconsistent across staff, where the same dish is framed differently by different servers in the same shift, producing a brand that reads as undecided.

### 10. Friction design

*Source: Thaler and Sunstein, Nudge; supported by Kahneman's framework*

#### Definition

Friction design is the deliberate addition or removal of effort at a specific point in a process. Friction can be added to slow a decision, prompt reflection, or signal value; it can be removed to speed an action that should not require thought.

#### Mechanism

Effort is a signal. When something is easy, people interpret the ease as a sign that the action is expected, low-stakes, and reversible. When something requires effort, they interpret the friction as a sign that the action is significant, deliberate, and worth considering. Friction can therefore be a design tool: removed where action should flow, added where reflection should occur. The two failure modes are friction in the wrong places (where it slows the customer down on things that should be effortless) and absence of friction in places where it would have served (where decisions are made too easily and regretted).

#### Hospitality application

Friction design is visible in many hospitality moments. The bill arrives quickly when the customer asks for it (friction removed); the bill is not pushed when the customer has not asked (friction preserved). The reservation system is easy to use (friction removed). The cancellation of a reservation requires a brief explanation (friction added — the customer reflects on whether they need to cancel, and the small effort is worth the no-show rate it prevents). The complimentary water comes without ordering (friction removed); the upgrade to a more expensive wine requires a moment of pause and a deliberate ask (friction preserved).

#### Worked illustration

A restaurant that automates its reservation cancellation — one click, no explanation — sees no-show rates rise. The same restaurant that requires the cancellation to be made by phone, with a brief conversation, sees no-show rates fall. The friction added at the cancellation point causes the customer to consider whether the cancellation is necessary; many bookings that would have lapsed are kept. The friction is not customer-hostile; it is decision-supportive.

#### Failure mode

Friction design fails in two directions. Too much friction on actions that should be effortless reads as the brand being difficult. Too little friction on actions that should require thought reads as the brand being commercial — one-click upgrades, defaults that quietly add charges, recovery flows that erase customer complaints with frictionless speed before the complaint has been heard. The design names the moments where friction belongs and removes it everywhere else.

### 11. Social proof

*Source: Cialdini, Influence*

#### Definition

Social proof is the principle by which people use the behaviour or judgement of others as input to their own decisions, particularly when their own information is incomplete or the situation is ambiguous.

#### Mechanism

When uncertain, people look to others. The looking is usually unconscious. Crowded restaurants signal quality; quiet restaurants signal risk. Tables ordering the same dish suggest the dish is the recommended one. Reviews, ratings, and visible signals of others' choices all operate as social proof. The strength depends on how similar the other people seem; proof from people the person identifies with carries more weight than proof from people they do not.

#### Hospitality application

Social proof operates from before the customer arrives — through reviews, reputation, and visible street activity — to throughout the visit. Within the room: how full the room is, what other tables are ordering, whether other customers seem to be enjoying themselves. Staff can engage social proof deliberately ("the table next to you just had the lamb and they loved it" — with caution, as this can read as commercial) or implicitly (the dish the kitchen is plating most often becomes visible and suggests itself).

#### Worked illustration

A restaurant's first weeks of operation are vulnerable to social proof: an empty room on a Tuesday night communicates risk to the few customers who do come in. A brand that anticipates this stages friends-and-family services aggressively in the first weeks, even at zero or negative margin, so the room appears worth being in. Once the room is regularly populated, the social proof becomes self-sustaining: customers walking past see a busy room, choose it, and the room remains busy. The intervention is the first three weeks of populating; after that, the principle does the work.

#### Failure mode

Social proof fails when it is manufactured. Staged reviews, paid plants in the room, and similar deceptions are noticed eventually and damage the brand more than the absence of social proof would have. The principle works on genuine signal; manufactured signal eventually inverts. A second failure mode: social proof used on audiences that are deliberately counter-cultural. A Sage audience or an Outlaw audience reads social proof negatively — what most people choose is, for them, an argument against the choice. The brand whose audience archetype resists social proof must use it sparingly or invert it.

### 12. Prospect theory — loss aversion and the endowment effect

*Source: Kahneman and Tversky, Prospect Theory; Thinking, Fast and Slow, Chapters 26-28*

#### Definition

Prospect theory is the Kahneman/Tversky model of how people actually decide under risk and uncertainty. Two of its central findings have direct hospitality application. Loss aversion: the psychological cost of losing something is roughly twice the pleasure of gaining the same thing. The endowment effect: once a person possesses something — even briefly — they value it more highly than they did before possession.

#### Mechanism

Gains and losses are not symmetrical in the mind. A possession, once held, becomes part of the person's reference point; giving it up registers as loss, weighted more heavily than the same value framed as not-yet-gained. The asymmetry shapes a wide range of decisions: people accept worse outcomes to avoid losses than to secure equivalent gains; they are slower to abandon something they already have than to forgo something they have not received.\\\\The endowment effect is the same asymmetry seen from the possession side. Trial periods, free samples, and "hold this for a moment" interactions all engage the endowment effect by giving the person temporary possession. The possessed item acquires a higher value, and giving it up registers as loss.

#### Hospitality application

Loss aversion operates in offer language, in upgrade architecture, and in retention. An offer framed as gain ("add a glass of wine for ten euros") is weaker than the same offer framed as preservation ("would you like to keep your pairing going — the next glass goes with the second course"). A loyalty mechanism framed as accumulating points is weaker than the same mechanism framed as preserving status already earned. Loss aversion also shapes recovery: a customer who has had something taken from them (a delayed dish, a wrong order) is owed more than a customer who never received it at all.\\\\The endowment effect operates in tasting, trial moments, and the design of choices where the customer briefly holds something before committing. A wine bottle placed on the table for the customer to read the label engages the effect — the customer has momentarily possessed the bottle. A dish placed in front of the customer for plating tableside engages the effect even before the first bite.

#### Worked illustration

A wine list presented with the sommelier bringing a bottle from the cellar to show the customer engages the endowment effect twice. The customer holds the bottle, reads the label, hands it back; the bottle is now associated with the customer's hands. The acceptance rate for the bottle the customer has held is higher than for the same bottle described from the list. The mechanism is not pressure — the customer is told they can decline — but the brief possession has shifted the bottle's value upward.\\\\Loss aversion at the upgrade: a restaurant offering a tasting menu add-on with the loss frame — "the chef has built a full tasting around tonight's produce — would you like that experience, or shall we stop at the four courses you've ordered" — positions the four-course as the partial experience and the eight-course as the complete one. Declining now means foregoing something the brand has named as available. Adoption rates rise.

#### Failure mode

Both fail when they cross into manipulation. Loss aversion fails when customers feel a framing is designed to make them feel a loss they would not naturally have felt; the recognition produces backlash. The endowment effect fails when the temporary possession is undone abruptly or with judgement — a customer who has tasted a wine and declined should be relieved of the bottle without comment. Both principles work as soft mechanisms; they break when they become hard ones.

### 13. Mental accounting

*Source: Thaler, Mental Accounting Matters; covered in Thinking, Fast and Slow, Chapter 32*

#### Definition

Mental accounting is the principle that people treat money differently depending on which category it sits in, where it came from, and what it is for. Money is not perfectly fungible in the mind; it is sorted into mental accounts that govern how it is spent and how spending is evaluated.

#### Mechanism

A person's relationship with the same hundred euros varies depending on the account it is in. Money in a vacation account is spent differently from money in a household budget. Money received unexpectedly is treated as windfall and spent more freely than equivalent salaried money. The cost of a dish at dinner is mentally accounted against the dinner's total cost, not against the person's overall finances. People spend more across a single dinner than they would on the same items consumed separately, because the meal becomes a single mental event.

#### Hospitality application

Mental accounting shapes pricing structures, bundle design, and the architecture of the bill. The cost of wine, against the cost of the dinner, is accounted as part of the dinner; this is why wine markup tolerates levels that look extreme as a standalone purchase. The cost of dessert against the cost of the meal is similar — the customer has already crossed the threshold of going out for dinner, and the marginal cost of dessert is mentally accounted against an already-committed meal.

#### Worked illustration

A restaurant offering a fixed-price tasting menu engages mental accounting deliberately. The customer accounts the whole evening against the tasting price, not against each course separately. The kitchen can include courses that would not have been ordered if priced individually (an amuse-bouche, a palate cleanser, a small additional course before dessert) because they are inside the mental account that has already been committed. A la carte ordering, by contrast, produces a more granular account in which each addition is judged against its individual price; uptake of palate cleansers and extras is lower.

#### Failure mode

Mental accounting fails when the mental accounts are violated unexpectedly. A bill that breaks down expected charges by line item in a way that surprises the customer (a cover charge not announced, a service charge not clear) disrupts the account and produces complaints out of proportion to the actual amount. The principle works when the customer's mental accounting is anticipated; it breaks when the bill arrives in a different shape than the customer was tracking.

### 14. Decision fatigue

*Source: Baumeister and colleagues; relates to System 2 depletion in Kahneman, Chapter 3*

#### Definition

Decision fatigue is the principle that the quality of decisions degrades as the number of decisions made within a period increases. After enough decisions, people default to the easiest option, accept poor outcomes to avoid further choosing, or stop choosing altogether.

#### Mechanism

Decision fatigue is System 2 depletion. Decision-making is a System 2 activity and System 2 has limited capacity. After a long string of decisions, the capacity is exhausted; subsequent decisions either default to System 1's easiest path or are avoided entirely. The effect is universal but varies in intensity by person and circumstance. Studies of judicial decisions, medical prescribing, and consumer choices all show degraded quality late in a decision-rich period compared with early in it.

#### Hospitality application

Decision fatigue operates throughout the hospitality arc. A customer arrives having made many decisions during the day; the restaurant adds more. The design's job is to minimise the decisions the customer has to make and to concentrate the meaningful decisions early, when capacity is highest. The end of the meal — when bills and digestifs are presented — is decision-fatigued territory; offerings here should be designed to require minimal effort.

#### Worked illustration

A restaurant facing low dessert orders investigates and finds that customers, at dessert-decision time, are tired and decline. The intervention is to remove the dessert as a choice and present it as a default: "we'll bring you a small dessert to share — let me know if you'd rather not." The decision is inverted from active (choose a dessert) to passive (decline a dessert). Dessert orders rise sharply. The kitchen's investment in pastry is rewarded; the customer's experience is more complete; no customer who genuinely does not want dessert is forced into one.

#### Failure mode

Decision fatigue mitigations fail when they cross into removing choices the customer wants to make. A customer who comes to a restaurant in part for the pleasure of choosing — a wine enthusiast, a curious diner — does not want defaults at the wine list. The mitigation is calibrated by audience: for the persona where decision-making is part of the experience, choices are preserved; for the persona where decision-making is friction, defaults are used.

### 15. Status quo bias

*Source: Samuelson and Zeckhauser; related to loss aversion (Kahneman/Tversky)*

#### Definition

Status quo bias is the tendency to prefer the current state — what one already has, the choices one has already made, the patterns one has already established — over change. The bias operates even when the change would be net beneficial.

#### Mechanism

Change requires effort and carries risk. The current state, by virtue of being current, has already been accepted and its outcomes are known. People weight the certainty of the current state more heavily than is rational, and accept ongoing low-grade dissatisfaction rather than disrupt a known pattern. The bias is closely related to loss aversion (the current state is a possession that change threatens) and to the endowment effect (the current state is owned and therefore valued).

#### Hospitality application

Status quo bias is the engine of customer loyalty. A customer who has chosen a restaurant once is more likely to choose it again, even when the second visit is not measurably better than alternatives. The first visit creates a status quo; the second visit confirms it; by the third visit the restaurant is the customer's default for that category of evening. The design implication: the first visit matters more than the second, the second more than the third. The early visits are when the status quo is being established.

#### Worked illustration

A restaurant treating first-visit customers and regular customers identically is leaving status quo bias unengaged. The same restaurant that recognises first-time customers and gives them a slightly elevated version of the standard experience — an extra moment of attention, a remembered preference noted, a personal note — establishes a status quo that the customer will preserve. The marginal cost is small; the loyalty effect is large.

#### Failure mode

Status quo bias fails when it is engaged for the wrong status quo. A customer who has had a negative first visit and not returned cannot be brought back through ordinary loyalty mechanisms. A second failure: status quo bias used as a reason to avoid changing things that should change. The brand's coherence is more important than any single regular's expectation; sometimes the status quo must be disrupted on purpose.

### 16. Hyperbolic discounting

*Source: Behavioural economics general; Ainslie, Picoeconomics*

#### Definition

Hyperbolic discounting is the tendency to weight immediate rewards disproportionately heavily compared to future rewards, even when the future reward is objectively larger. The discount rate is not consistent — it is steeper for near-term comparisons than for distant ones.

#### Mechanism

A person offered a small reward now and a larger reward later will often take the smaller one immediately, even though they would, in the abstract, prefer the larger reward. The bias is steepest at the margin between immediate and slightly-delayed. The mechanism is part impatience, part uncertainty (the immediate reward is certain; the future is not), part loss aversion against waiting.

#### Hospitality application

Hyperbolic discounting operates in moments where the customer is choosing between immediate gratification and a deferred but greater pleasure. The dessert now versus the digestif later. The second cocktail now versus the wine pairing with the main course. The check-now-and-leave versus the linger-over-coffee. The design at each of these moments can either work with the bias (offering the immediate pleasure as the default) or against it (slowing the moment so the deferred pleasure has time to register).

#### Worked illustration

A restaurant whose strongest moments are late in the meal — a dessert programme, an after-dinner digestif programme, a coffee-with-petit-fours moment — risks losing customers to hyperbolic discounting if the mid-meal pacing is too quick. The customer, halfway through, is being asked implicitly whether to commit to the back half or wrap up. The design slows the mid-meal pace deliberately to allow the back-half pleasures to come into view, framing them not as additional commitments but as the meal's natural completion. Customers who would have asked for the bill at the end of the main course are instead carried through to dessert.

#### Failure mode

Hyperbolic discounting cannot be designed against entirely — sometimes the customer simply wants to leave. The principle is a lens, not a lever. The mitigation works on the margins, with customers who would have stayed for the back-half pleasures with a small assist; it does not work on customers who have already decided the evening is finished.

### 17. Thin-slicing

*Source: Gladwell, Blink; Gottman, Ambady, and others*

#### Definition

Thin-slicing is the cognitive ability to form remarkably accurate judgements from very brief observations — sometimes as short as a few seconds. The judgements are made by System 1, often outside awareness, and are typically resistant to revision by subsequent contradicting information.

#### Mechanism

Gladwell, drawing on research by Gottman, Ambady, and others, documents that humans can predict marital outcomes from three minutes of conversation, teaching quality from two seconds of muted video, surgical malpractice risk from voice tone alone. The mechanism is System 1's pattern-recognition operating on cues that the conscious mind does not register. The accuracy of thin-slicing is sometimes striking; the inputs to it are largely opaque to the person making the judgement.\\\\Thin-slicing is also the source of certain systematic errors — what Gladwell calls the Warren Harding error, where appearance-based snap judgements produce confident but wrong conclusions (Harding was elected partly because he looked like a president; he was one of the worst). The same mechanism that produces accurate first impressions can produce confident bias.

#### Hospitality application

Thin-slicing is hospitality's threshold problem. Customers form impressions of the brand within seconds of entering — often before any staff member has spoken. The first impression is largely complete by the time the host says the first word. The impression is formed from cues the customer is not aware of registering: the room's smell, the ambient sound, the lighting's emotional register, the staff's body language as the customer crosses the threshold, the way the door closes, the rhythm of the room. These cues are processed in parallel by System 1 and produce a snap judgement that anchors everything that follows.\\\\Because the impression is formed so fast and from cues the customer cannot articulate, asking customers what made the first impression is largely uninformative. The customer cannot tell. The cues that did the work were not consciously perceived. The design must work the cues directly, not via the customer's stated preferences.

#### Worked illustration

Two restaurants in the same neighbourhood. One has invested in the threshold: a host stand positioned for arrival sightlines, lighting calibrated for the room's first signal, a soundtrack at a precise volume, a scent profile that registers without dominating, staff trained in the locked greeting at an exact tone. The other has these elements but undesigned — host stand wherever the room allowed it, lighting at standard restaurant levels, music on whatever the manager chose tonight, staff greeting in their personal style. The food, the menu, the price are identical. The first restaurant's customers thin-slice favourably; the second's thin-slice neutrally. Reviews diverge predictably even though only the threshold differs.

#### Failure mode

Thin-slicing fails when the brand designs the conscious first impression and neglects the unconscious one. The customer who walks in and notices the welcome message on the wall has had their conscious attention engaged; the cues that actually formed the snap judgement (smell, sound, light, body language) operated before the conscious attention. Design that addresses only what the customer notices is design that misses the actual mechanism. A second failure mode is the Warren Harding error — staff hired for appearance rather than character produce snap judgements that work in the candidate's favour without serving the brand. The brand's hiring discipline must address this: the audience's thin-slice of staff is real and inevitable, but the staff selected for the brand are selected on character that the customer's subsequent experience will confirm.

## 3.1.6 The limits of asking customers

One principle in this library bears separately on hospitality practice and deserves its own short treatment: the systematic unreliability of asking customers what they think.

The unreliability has three sources, treated by both Kahneman and Gladwell.

#### Source 1 — Substitution

As covered in principle 2, System 1 substitutes hard questions for easy ones. "How was the meal?" becomes "How did the last few minutes feel?" The customer answers the easier question believing they have answered the original. Customer satisfaction surveys are particularly vulnerable to this — the survey question is hard, the substituted question is easy, the answer reports on the substitute.

#### Source 2 — The locked door

Gladwell's phrase for the principle that people cannot reliably access the reasons for their own reactions. The customer who liked the room cannot say why. The customer who was uncomfortable with the staff cannot say what specifically was wrong. The reactions are System 1 outputs; the conscious mind has only the reaction, not its cause. When asked to explain, the customer constructs an explanation that sounds plausible — but the construction is post-hoc and often wrong.

This is not the customer being dishonest. They are doing their best with information they do not have. The conscious mind is trying to explain the unconscious mind's verdict, with limited success.

#### Source 3 — Kenna's dilemma

Gladwell's case study: the musician Kenna received enthusiastic responses from industry insiders who heard him perform, from sophisticated audiences in clubs, from MTV viewers who watched his videos repeatedly. He received poor scores from market research firms that asked respondents to rate his songs on numerical scales. The disagreement was not about Kenna's quality. It was about what asking does to a judgement.

When customers are asked to evaluate something, they engage System 2 — they think about why they liked it, weigh it against alternatives, consider what they would say. The act of evaluating distorts the evaluation. The judgement that would have emerged from direct experience is overwritten by the judgement that emerges from deliberate reflection — and the two are different. Music research that asks people to rate songs systematically under-predicts the success of innovative songs, because innovation is not what System 2 chooses on reflection.

The hospitality version of Kenna's dilemma: customers asked at the end of the meal to rate the experience produce ratings that bear only limited relationship to whether they will return. The remembering self decides return; the asked-to-rate self produces a number that reflects something else.

Implications for hospitality

Three implications for the practice.

First: customer feedback is not data about the experience. It is data about the customer's verbal report of the experience, mediated by substitution, the locked door, and the act of asking. Both kinds of data are useful for different purposes, but they are not interchangeable.

Second: focus groups and surveys that ask customers what they want or what they liked produce systematically biased answers. The biases are predictable. Customers under-report the importance of unconscious cues (the smell, the lighting, the staff's body language) and over-report the importance of conscious ones (the menu language, the bill format). Design that follows what customers say they want will under-invest in the cues that actually drive their satisfaction.

Third: behavioural observation outperforms verbal feedback for understanding what is actually happening. Watching whether customers linger over dessert is better evidence about dessert than asking them how they liked it. Watching whether tables look around the room with interest in the first five minutes is better evidence about the room than asking what they thought of the decor. Observation accesses the experiencing self; questions access the remembering self mediated by the asked-to-rate self.

The practice's discipline is to design from the experiencing self (what the customer actually goes through) toward the remembering self (what they will recall), with the observation that what customers say about either is partial. The brand book, the journey map, and the journey design are the practice's instruments for substituting deliberate design for asking customers what they want.

## 3.1.7 Using more than one principle at a moment

Most moments engage more than one principle. Ordering involves anchoring (the menu's price hierarchy), defaults (what the server suggests if asked), choice architecture (the menu's grouping and visual hierarchy), framing (the language of the descriptions), decision fatigue (where in the meal it falls), and several others. The design does not attempt to engage all of them deliberately. The design names the two or three that are dominant at this moment and addresses those; the others are present but secondary.

Two heuristics for which principles to focus on at any moment.

First: what is the customer's primary cognitive task at this moment, and is it a System 1 task or a System 2 task? If System 1 dominates (a glance, a feeling, a snap judgement), the relevant principles are cognitive ease, affect heuristic, thin-slicing, anchoring, defaults. If System 2 is engaged (a choice, a comparison, a decision), the relevant principles are choice architecture, framing, anchoring (the System 2 adjustment side), and loss aversion.

Second: what is the brand's intention at this moment. If the brand wants the customer to choose well from a wide menu, choice architecture is central. If the brand wants the customer to accept a recommendation, anchoring and defaults are central. If the brand wants the customer to come back, the two selves framework is central — peak-end and the remembering self govern return. If the brand wants the customer to recall the experience warmly to friends, affect heuristic and peak-end govern the recall.

Combining principles is not the same as stacking them. A moment that uses anchoring and defaults and choice architecture and framing and social proof and loss aversion all at once reads as commercial in a way that breaks the brand. The discipline is to pick the principles the moment needs and to use them with restraint. Most well-designed moments use two principles deliberately; the third and beyond are usually unnecessary.

A final note on this library and the brand. Behavioural economics is a set of mechanisms, not a set of values. Mechanisms can be used to serve customers or to exploit them. The brand book governs which. A brand whose Connection statement reads "I can guide you to the answer" uses these principles to guide; a brand whose Connection statement reads "I will sell you what makes me the most money" would use them to extract — but such a brand has no place in the practice. Where the principles serve the brand's stated relationship with the customer, they are used. Where they would betray it, they are not.
